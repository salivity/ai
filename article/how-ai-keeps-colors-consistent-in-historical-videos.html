How AI Keeps Colors Consistent in Historical Videos

Artificial intelligence video colorization networks maintain consistent clothing and object hues across thousands of historical black-and-white film frames by combining spatial feature recognition with temporal tracking. Instead of treating a video as a series of isolated photographs, advanced neural networks analyze the motion of pixels, utilize reference frames, and apply long-term memory models to ensure a blue jacket or a red car remains the exact same shade from the beginning of a scene to the end. This article explains the core mechanisms—such as optical flow, recurrent neural networks, and attention modules—that prevent color flickering and maintain visual continuity in restored footage.

The Challenge of Temporal Consistency

When AI colorizes a single black-and-white photograph, it guesses colors based on context, such as making the sky blue or grass green. However, applying this same method frame-by-frame to a video results in severe flickering. An AI might color a person's shirt blue in frame one, purple in frame two, and grey in frame three due to slight changes in lighting or posture. To solve this, networks must enforce "temporal consistency," which is the ability to link the identity of an object across time.

Optical Flow and Motion Tracking

The foundational method AI uses to keep colors steady is optical flow. Optical flow algorithms calculate the motion of every pixel from one frame to the next.

  • Pixel Mapping: The AI identifies that a specific cluster of pixels representing a hat in frame one has moved three millimeters to the right in frame two.
  • Color Propagation: Instead of guessing the color of the hat all over again in the second frame, the AI simply carries the color data over along the calculated path of motion.
  • Occlusion Handling: If an object temporarily moves behind something else (occlusion), advanced optical flow models detect when the object reappears and reapply the previously assigned hue.

Reference-Based Colorization (Exemplars)

To maintain consistency across thousands of frames, many AI models use a reference-based approach. The network selects or is given a single colorized "keyframe" at the start of a scene.

As the video plays, the AI constantly looks back at this original reference frame. It extracts semantic features—meaning it recognizes shapes like faces, cars, and dresses—and matches the objects in the current black-and-white frame to the objects in the colored reference frame. Even if a person turns around or walks into a shadow, the AI links the current view of the clothing to the reference image, locking the hue in place.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Memory

For long historical clips, looking only at the previous frame is not enough. Gradual color drift can occur, where a red coat slowly shifts to orange over several minutes. To prevent this, developers use Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), specifically Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and Vision Transformers.

These networks possess an internal memory state. As the AI processes the video, it stores a mathematical summary of the colors and objects it has already seen. When processing frame 2,000, the AI accesses its memory of frames 1 through 1,999. This global awareness ensures that if a character leaves the room and returns five minutes later, the AI remembers the exact color of their suit and applies it accurately.

Temporal Loss Functions During Training

AI networks learn to avoid flickering during their training phase through a mathematical rule called a "temporal loss function." During training, the AI is given a color video that has been artificially turned black-and-white. As the AI attempts to re-color it, the system penalizes the network not just for picking the wrong color, but for changing colors between consecutive frames. By heavily penalizing frame-to-frame variations, the neural network learns that keeping a hue stable is just as important as picking a realistic color in the first place.

Self-Attention and Transformers

The newest generation of video colorization AI relies on Transformer architectures, which use a mechanism called "self-attention." Instead of processing a video strictly in chronological order, Transformers can analyze blocks of hundreds of frames simultaneously. The AI builds a 3D map of the video (width, height, and time). By viewing an object across a massive block of time all at once, the network applies a single, unified color decision to the entire duration of that object's appearance, completely eliminating color shifts.
