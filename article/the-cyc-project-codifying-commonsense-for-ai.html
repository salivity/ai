The Cyc Project: Codifying Commonsense for AI

This article explores the Cyc project, the longest-running artificial intelligence initiative dedicated to giving machines human-like commonsense reasoning. Launched in 1984 by Douglas Lenat, the project aims to overcome the brittleness of traditional AI by manually encoding millions of everyday facts and rules of thumb into a massive knowledge base. By examining Cyc's unique representation language, its inference engine, and the use of partitioned contexts, this piece breaks down the methods used to translate unwritten human assumptions into machine-readable logic.

The Core Problem: Unstated Assumptions

Traditional artificial intelligence excels at narrow, well-defined tasks like playing chess or calculating data, yet it routinely fails at understanding the basic mechanics of the physical and social world. Humans rely on a vast reservoir of unstated background knowledge—such as the fact that water is wet, trees are usually outdoors, and people cannot be in two places at once. Because humans find these facts obvious, they rarely write them down. Consequently, AI models trained solely on text or raw data often miss the foundational truths required for genuine comprehension. The Cyc project was created to bridge this gap by explicitly writing down the rules of reality.

CycL: The Language of Commonsense

To store this knowledge, the creators of Cyc realized that natural languages like English were too ambiguous, and standard programming languages were too rigid. They developed CycL, a formal representation language based on higher-order predicate calculus.

In CycL, concepts are represented as "constants" (such as # $Dog or # $Sleeping). Engineers, known as "ontologists," use these constants to write logical statements, called assertions, that define how the world works. For example, instead of just matching keywords, CycL encodes the strict logical rule that if an entity is a mammal, it must have a biological mother. Over decades, human researchers have manually input over 25 million of these assertions into the system.

Microtheories: Handling Contradictions

A major challenge in codifying commonsense is that rules change depending on the context. For example, the statement "vampires do not exist" is true in the real world, but false in the context of Dracula's universe. Similarly, the rules of physics we use in everyday life differ from the rules of quantum mechanics.

Cyc solves this problem using "microtheories." A microtheory is a specific context or domain within the knowledge base where a certain set of assertions holds true. By partitioning knowledge into thousands of different microtheories—ranging from "Human Social Life" to "Naive Physics" to "Star Wars Lore"—Cyc can hold contradictory beliefs without breaking its logical system. When solving a problem, the AI simply activates the relevant microtheories for that specific situation.

The Inference Engine: Connecting the Dots

Having a massive database of facts is useless if the AI cannot connect them to draw new conclusions. Paired with the Cyc knowledge base is a powerful inference engine. This engine uses deductive reasoning—such as syllogisms, modus ponens, and inheritance—to chain together separate pieces of knowledge.

If you ask Cyc whether a person can drown in a cup of coffee, it does not need a pre-written rule specifically about coffee cups and drowning. Instead, the inference engine combines multiple facts: drowning requires submerging a person's airways in liquid, a human head is larger than a standard coffee cup, and a larger object cannot be fully submerged inside a smaller object. Through this logical deduction, Cyc concludes that it is impossible.

The Legacy of Symbolic AI

While modern AI is heavily dominated by machine learning and neural networks that learn patterns from vast amounts of data, Cyc represents the pinnacle of "symbolic AI." By prioritizing explicit rules and human-curated logic, the Cyc project guarantees explainability; unlike a neural network, Cyc can show the exact chain of logic it used to reach a conclusion. Today, Cyc continues to operate commercially, often working alongside machine learning models to provide the logical guardrails and commonsense reasoning that statistical systems still lack.
