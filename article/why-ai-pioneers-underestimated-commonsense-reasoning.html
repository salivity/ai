Why AI Pioneers Underestimated Commonsense Reasoning

Early artificial intelligence researchers believed that creating a fully thinking machine would only take a few decades, largely because they vastly underestimated the complexity of commonsense reasoning. While pioneers quickly succeeded in programming computers to solve complex calculus problems and play chess, they failed to realize that intuitive, everyday human knowledge is exponentially harder to codify. This article explores the core reasons behind this miscalculation, including the illusion of Moravec's Paradox, the limits of formal logic, the frame problem, and the hidden vastness of human tacit knowledge.

The Illusion of Moravec’s Paradox

In the 1950s and 1960s, AI researchers assumed that tasks difficult for humans—like playing grandmaster-level chess or proving mathematical theorems—would be the hardest for computers. Conversely, they assumed tasks easy for a human toddler—like recognizing a face or understanding that water is wet—would be trivial to program.

This misconception is now known as Moravec’s Paradox. Researchers did not realize that "easy" human skills are actually the product of millions of years of evolutionary fine-tuning. Our brains process vast amounts of sensory data effortlessly and unconsciously. Because humans do not have to consciously think about how they apply common sense, researchers mistakenly believed the underlying computational processes were simple.

The Limits of Symbolic AI and Formal Logic

Early AI was dominated by an approach called "Symbolic AI" or "Good Old-Fashioned AI" (GOFAI). Pioneers believed that all human intelligence could be reduced to the manipulation of symbols using strict rules of formal logic.

While formal logic works brilliantly in closed, rule-based environments like math or board games, it collapses in the real world. Commonsense reasoning is rarely strict; it is fuzzy, adaptable, and full of exceptions. For example, a logical rule might state, "Birds can fly." However, a human instantly knows this does not apply to penguins, ostriches, dead birds, or birds with broken wings. Early researchers underestimated the sheer number of exceptions required to navigate everyday reality.

The Infinite Vastness of Tacit Knowledge

Humans navigate the world using "tacit knowledge"—unspoken, unwritten assumptions about how the physical and social worlds operate. We know that you can pull a string to move an object, but you cannot push a string to do the same. We know that if a person walks into a room, their head comes with them.

Because this knowledge is so obvious, humans never explicitly state it. Early AI researchers did not realize how massive this database of unwritten facts actually was. When projects like Cyc attempted to manually code all human common sense in the 1980s, researchers discovered it required millions of complex rules just to get a computer to understand basic concepts of time, space, and causality.

The Frame Problem

A major roadblock that caught early researchers off guard was the "Frame Problem." In AI, this refers to the challenge of telling a computer what does not change after an action takes place.

If a human paints a table red, they automatically know that the table's weight, the location of the house, and the current president remain unchanged. Early AI systems, however, had to logically evaluate every single fact in their database to see if painting the table affected it. Researchers struggled to figure out how to give machines the commonsense intuition to ignore irrelevant information, leading to massive computational bottlenecks.

Lack of Physical Embodiment

Early AI programs were entirely disembodied, existing only as code on mainframe computers. Researchers underestimated how much of human common sense is derived from having a physical body that interacts with the material world.

Humans understand concepts like gravity, friction, containment, and spatial awareness because we spend our infancy dropping toys, crawling, and bumping into furniture. By trying to teach computers common sense purely through text and abstract symbols, early pioneers were trying to teach a machine about a physical reality it had no way of experiencing.
