Visualizing AI Embeddings with PCA and UMAP

This article provides a concise explanation of dimensionality reduction and its vital role in interpreting artificial intelligence data. It explores the nature of high-dimensional AI embeddings, defines dimensionality reduction, and breaks down how Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) translate complex data into clear, visual formats that humans can easily understand.

Understanding High-Dimensional AI Embeddings

Artificial intelligence models process complex information—such as text, images, and audio—by converting it into numerical representations called embeddings. An embedding is a long list of numbers, also known as a vector, that captures the meaning and features of the original data.

For example, a language model might represent a single word or sentence using a vector containing 1,536 different numbers. In data science, each of these numbers represents a "dimension." While AI models easily calculate relationships across thousands of dimensions, the human brain cannot visualize anything beyond three dimensions. To inspect how an AI groups similar concepts together, we must compress these dimensions.

What is Dimensionality Reduction?

Dimensionality reduction is the mathematical process of taking data with many dimensions (features) and compressing it into fewer dimensions—typically two or three—while preserving as much of the original information and structure as possible.

Think of dimensionality reduction like casting a shadow. A 3D object, such as your hand, is projected onto a 2D wall as a shadow. While you lose some depth information, the 2D shadow still retains enough structural data for you to recognize the shape of a hand. Dimensionality reduction does the exact same thing, compressing thousands of dimensions down to a 2D scatter plot or a 3D graph.

How PCA Works: Preserving Global Structure

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is one of the oldest and most widely used dimensionality reduction techniques. It is a linear method that focuses on capturing the "big picture" of the data.

PCA works by finding the directions in the data that have the largest variance (spread).

  • Principal Component 1: PCA draws a straight line through the high-dimensional data that captures the maximum possible spread of the points.
  • Principal Component 2: It then draws a second straight line, perpendicular to the first, that captures the second most significant spread.

By projecting the data onto just these two lines, PCA creates a 2D chart.

How PCA helps visualize embeddings:

  • Speed: PCA is computationally fast and efficient, making it ideal for a quick first look at massive AI datasets.
  • Global Preservation: It excels at preserving global structure, meaning points that are very far apart in the high-dimensional space will remain far apart in the 2D visualization.
  • Limitation: Because PCA only uses straight lines (linear projections), it often fails to capture complex, curved relationships within the data, causing tight clusters of similar embeddings to overlap and look like a single blurry cloud.

How UMAP Works: Preserving Local Neighborhoods

Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) is a modern, non-linear dimensionality reduction technique. Unlike PCA, which looks at the broadest spread of the data, UMAP focuses on the intricate relationships between neighboring points.

UMAP operates on the mathematical assumption that the high-dimensional data lies on a curved surface (a manifold) that can be unfolded.

  • Step 1: UMAP looks at every data point in the high-dimensional space and connects it to its closest neighbors, building a complex web of local relationships.
  • Step 2: It then recreates a similar web in a 2D or 3D space, pulling connected points together and pushing unconnected points apart until the low-dimensional layout closely matches the high-dimensional web.

How UMAP helps visualize embeddings:

  • Cluster Separation: Because it is non-linear, UMAP can unfold complex data structures. It excels at grouping similar AI embeddings into tight, distinct islands on a chart.
  • Balance: While UMAP prioritizes local structure (keeping similar items together), it also preserves much of the global structure (keeping different clusters appropriately spaced relative to one another).
  • Use Case: If you want to see how an AI categorizes different topics—such as separating medical articles from sports articles—UMAP will clearly show these distinct groups, whereas PCA might blend them together.

Choosing Between PCA and UMAP

When visualizing AI embeddings, data scientists often use both tools in tandem. PCA is used first to rapidly reduce an embedding from 1,500 dimensions down to 50, stripping away noise and speeding up calculations. UMAP is then applied to those 50 dimensions to compress them into the final 2D or 3D visual map, revealing the hidden clusters and patterns learned by the artificial intelligence.
