AI and Multispectral Imaging in Burn Trauma Surgery

Burn trauma surgeons increasingly rely on multispectral tissue reflectance imaging combined with artificial intelligence to accurately assess burn wound depth. This article explores how these technologies work together to differentiate between partial-thickness burns capable of spontaneous healing and deep burns that require surgical skin grafting. By examining light reflectance across multiple wavelengths and applying machine learning algorithms, medical teams can make faster, more precise treatment decisions, ultimately improving patient recovery and reducing unnecessary surgeries.

Visual inspection is the traditional method for evaluating burn depth, yet even experienced surgeons only achieve an accuracy rate of 60% to 75% when distinguishing between superficial and deep partial-thickness burns. Superficial partial-thickness burns retain enough viable dermal tissue and blood supply to heal on their own within two to three weeks. Conversely, deep partial-thickness and full-thickness burns have destroyed the dermal vascular plexus and epidermal regeneration sources, necessitating surgical excision and skin grafting to prevent severe scarring and infection. Because visual cues like blistering and tissue color change over the first 72 hours, clinical assessments are often delayed, postponing critical surgical intervention.

Multispectral tissue reflectance imaging overcomes the limits of human vision by capturing data beyond the visible light spectrum. The imaging device shines specific wavelengths of visible and near-infrared light onto the burn wound and measures the light reflected back by the tissue. Different components of the skin absorb and scatter light in predictable ways. For example, oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin in the blood absorb specific wavelengths, indicating the level of blood flow and vascular damage. Meanwhile, water content reveals tissue edema, and the scattering of light indicates the structural integrity of dermal collagen fibers. By capturing these invisible physiological markers, the scanner creates a comprehensive optical profile of the damaged tissue beneath the surface.

Wound-depth artificial intelligence translates this complex optical data into actionable clinical insights. Machine learning models, particularly convolutional neural networks, are trained on thousands of multispectral burn images paired with their known clinical outcomes or biopsy results. When a surgeon scans a new burn, the AI analyzes the reflectance patterns pixel by pixel. It identifies subtle signatures of tissue viability—such as preserved microcirculation and intact deep collagen—that indicate a burn will regenerate spontaneously. Simultaneously, it detects zones of vascular thrombosis and denatured proteins characteristic of non-viable tissue.

In the trauma bay or burn unit, the integration of these technologies provides immediate point-of-care diagnostics. A handheld or cart-based scanner captures the multispectral image in seconds without touching the patient, preventing pain and contamination. The AI processes the reflectance data and generates a color-coded map overlaid directly onto a standard photograph of the wound. This map clearly outlines areas of spontaneous healing versus areas requiring excision and grafting.

With this precise mapping, burn surgeons no longer have to wait days for a burn wound to declare itself visually. They can perform early tangential excision and apply skin grafts exclusively to the non-viable areas identified by the AI, while preserving adjacent tissue that the system predicts will heal on its own. This targeted approach preserves healthy skin, minimizes blood loss, shortens hospital stays, and significantly improves long-term functional and cosmetic outcomes for burn patients.
