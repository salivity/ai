Clearing Titanium Hip Streaks in CT Scans

X-ray computed tomography (CT) scans often suffer from severe streaking and shadowing when imaging patients with titanium hip implants. These distortions, known as metal artifacts, obscure critical anatomical structures and hinder accurate diagnosis. Modern imaging overcomes this limitation by combining two advanced techniques: dual-energy physics and artificial intelligence (AI) sinogram inpainting. Dual-energy physics addresses the root cause of the distortion by manipulating X-ray energy spectra, while AI algorithms directly repair the corrupted raw data before the final image is constructed, resulting in a clear, diagnostic-quality scan.

The Challenge of Titanium Hip Implants

To understand how artifacts are removed, it is necessary to understand how they form. Standard CT scanners emit a polyenergetic X-ray beam, meaning the beam contains photons of varying energy levels. As this beam passes through a dense titanium hip implant, the lower-energy photons are completely absorbed, while only the high-energy photons pass through.

This phenomenon causes two primary issues:

  • Beam Hardening: The average energy of the X-ray beam increases (hardens) as it passes through the metal, violating the mathematical assumptions used to reconstruct the CT image and creating dark streaks.
  • Photon Starvation: In particularly thick areas of the hip prosthesis, an insufficient number of photons reach the CT detector, leading to high noise and bright streaks radiating from the implant.

Dual-Energy Physics

Dual-energy CT (DECT) utilizes the principles of physics to mitigate beam hardening. Instead of scanning the patient with a single X-ray energy spectrum, DECT acquires data using two distinct energy levels—typically one low (e.g., 80 kilovolt peak) and one high (e.g., 140 kilovolt peak).

Because different materials absorb X-rays differently at various energy levels, the scanner's software can compare the two datasets to mathematically calculate what the tissue would look like if it had been scanned by a single, high-energy X-ray beam. This generates a Virtual Monoenergetic Image (VMI). By reconstructing the VMI at a high energy level (typically between 100 and 150 kiloelectron volts), the beam hardening effect caused by the titanium is largely neutralized, significantly reducing dark shading around the hip joint.

AI-Driven Sinogram Inpainting

While dual-energy physics handles beam hardening well, it cannot fully resolve photon starvation, where data is completely missing. This requires intervention in the raw data domain. Before a CT scan becomes a recognizable cross-sectional image, the raw data collected by the detectors exists as a "sinogram"—a wave-like pattern representing the X-ray projections from every angle.

Sinogram inpainting treats the data corrupted by the titanium implant as a damaged region that needs to be erased and redrawn. The process works in three distinct steps:

  1. Metal Segmentation: The system identifies the exact boundaries of the titanium implant and traces its path (the "metal trace") within the raw sinogram data.
  2. Data Removal: The corrupted data inside the metal trace is deleted, leaving a blank gap in the sinogram.
  3. AI Inpainting: Deep learning models, specifically Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) or Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), analyze the intact data surrounding the gap. Having been trained on thousands of artifact-free scans, the AI accurately predicts and fills in the missing sinogram waves, ensuring smooth, physically accurate transitions across the deleted zone.

The Combined Workflow

When used together, dual-energy physics and AI sinogram inpainting provide a comprehensive Metal Artifact Reduction (MAR) solution. Dual-energy acquisitions first minimize the physical errors of beam hardening, providing the AI with a cleaner baseline sinogram. The AI then steps in to seamlessly inpaint the remaining areas where the titanium hip implant completely blocked the X-ray photons. Once the sinogram is repaired, the scanner reconstructs the data into a final image, revealing the bone-implant interface and surrounding soft tissues of the pelvis without obstructing streaks.
