Normalizing Relative Dates in AI Temporal Extraction

This article explores how Artificial Intelligence uses Temporal Information Extraction (TIE) to convert relative date expressions, such as "last Tuesday," into standardized ISO timestamps for timeline graphs. It covers the end-to-end pipeline, beginning with the identification of temporal phrases in unstructured text and the establishment of reference anchor dates. Furthermore, it details the computational logic behind resolving offsets into canonical formats and mapping these unified data points onto chronological knowledge graphs.

Temporal Expression Recognition (TER)

The normalization process begins with identifying that a relative date exists within the text. Natural Language Processing (NLP) models scan unstructured text to locate temporal mentions, often referred to as "timex" (temporal expressions). Using transformer-based architectures or Named Entity Recognition (NER) pipelines, the AI tags phrases like "yesterday," "three weeks ago," or "last Tuesday" as relative temporal entities rather than static dates.

Establishing the Anchor Time

A relative expression is meaningless without a point of reference. To resolve "last Tuesday," the AI must first determine the Anchor Time, most commonly the Document Creation Time (DCT).

In simple scenarios, the DCT is extracted from the file's metadata or a timestamp on a message. However, in complex narratives, the anchor time might shift dynamically based on the text itself. If a paragraph begins with "On May 14, 2023, John arrived in Paris," the AI sets this explicit date as the local anchor time for subsequent relative expressions in that passage.

Temporal Resolution and Calculation

Once the relative expression and the anchor time are isolated, the AI performs temporal resolution. This involves translating linguistic constructs into mathematical operations.

  1. Parsing the Linguistic Structure: The system breaks down "last Tuesday." The word "last" is interpreted as a backward directional modifier (a negative offset), and "Tuesday" is mapped to a numerical day of the week (where Monday is 1 and Tuesday is 2, per ISO standards).
  2. Applying Calendar Arithmetic: The AI checks the day of the week of the anchor time. If the anchor time is Friday, October 20, 2023 (Day 5), the system calculates the difference needed to reach the previous Tuesday (Day 2).
  3. Executing the Offset: Using either rule-based systems (like HeidelTime or SUTime) or neural sequence-to-sequence models, the engine subtracts the calculated days from the anchor date.

Formatting to Canonical ISO-8601

After the exact date is calculated, it is converted into a canonical ISO-8601 timestamp (typically YYYY-MM-DD or YYYY-MM-DDTHH:MM:SSZ). Following the previous example, "last Tuesday" relative to Friday, October 20, 2023, is resolved and formatted to 2023-10-17.

During this phase, the system often adheres to the TimeML specification, an standard markup language for temporal events. The raw text "last Tuesday" is wrapped in a tag containing its resolved value, bridging the gap between human language and machine-readable data.

Mapping to Timeline Graphs

The final step is integrating the canonical timestamp into a timeline graph (or Temporal Knowledge Graph). In these graphs, events and entities are represented as nodes, while relationships and temporal sequences are represented as edges.

By replacing the ambiguous string "last Tuesday" with the canonical node or attribute 2023-10-17, the AI can accurately plot the event on a linear timeline. This allows the graph database to execute precise chronological queries, infer causality (determining if Event A happened before Event B), and merge information from multiple documents written on different dates into a single, unified timeline.
