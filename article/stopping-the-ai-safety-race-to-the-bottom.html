Stopping the AI Safety Race to the Bottom

This article examines the strategies international governance bodies can deploy to prevent nations from lowering artificial intelligence safety standards in pursuit of competitive advantages. It covers the establishment of binding global treaties, the implementation of hardware-based verification mechanisms, the creation of international auditing agencies, and the use of economic incentives and trade barriers to enforce compliance. By integrating these regulatory frameworks, the global community can foster technological innovation without compromising human safety or geopolitical stability.

Establish Binding International Treaties

Voluntary commitments are insufficient when national security and economic dominance are at stake. International governance bodies must transition from non-binding guidelines to enforceable global treaties, similar to those governing nuclear non-proliferation or chemical weapons. These treaties must define clear red lines for AI development, such as prohibiting autonomous weapons systems and models capable of self-replication or cyber-offense without human oversight. Signatory nations would be legally required to codify these international baselines into their domestic laws, ensuring that no country can legally undercut global safety protocols to speed up deployment.

Create an International AI Auditing Agency

Rules are ineffective without verification. An independent body, modeled after the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), is necessary to monitor compliance. This agency would have the authority to conduct confidential, rigorous safety evaluations of frontier AI models before they are trained and deployed. By standardizing red-teaming exercises—where experts attempt to find flaws and vulnerabilities in AI systems—the agency can certify whether a national program meets global safety thresholds. Crucially, this can be achieved using secure, privacy-preserving auditing techniques that verify safety without exposing proprietary algorithms or national secrets.

Implement Compute Governance and Hardware Tracking

Advanced AI development relies heavily on a highly concentrated supply chain of specialized semiconductors and massive data centers. International governance bodies can leverage this bottleneck to enforce safety standards. By working with the few nations and corporations that manufacture advanced AI chips, regulators can implement "know-your-customer" (KYC) protocols for compute infrastructure. Access to the most powerful hardware should be contingent upon a nation's adherence to international safety standards. Furthermore, hardware-level mechanisms can be built into chips to verify if they are being used for unauthorized, large-scale training runs that violate safety treaties.

Align Economic Incentives and Trade Access

To prevent nations from defecting from safety agreements, international bodies must tie compliance directly to economic benefits. Global trade organizations can establish rules where access to international markets for AI products and services is restricted to nations that adhere to certified safety standards. If a country attempts to cut corners on safety to accelerate its AI program, its technologies should face immediate tariffs, sanctions, or outright bans in compliant markets. Conversely, nations that adhere to rigorous safety standards should gain access to shared international research pools, funding, and safe-harbor legal protections.

Foster Collaborative Safety Research

A race to the bottom often occurs because safety is viewed as a tax on speed. International bodies can change this dynamic by pooling global resources to fund Joint AI Safety Institutes. By treating safety alignment research as a global public good and sharing breakthroughs openly among compliant nations, the cost and time required to make AI safe are drastically reduced. When safety tools and alignment techniques are freely available and easily integrated, nations have less incentive to bypass them in the pursuit of rapid development.
