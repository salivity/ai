Solomonoff Induction and the Universal Prior in AI

This article explores how Solomonoff induction establishes a mathematical foundation for idealized artificial intelligence by solving the problem of inductive inference. It explains how the framework combines Kolmogorov complexity and algorithmic probability to create a universal prior, formally integrating Occam's razor and Epicurus's principle of multiple explanations into Bayesian updating to predict future data from past observations.

The Challenge of Inductive Inference

In artificial intelligence, inductive inference is the process of predicting future data based on past observations. To make accurate predictions using Bayesian probability, an AI agent needs a "prior"—an initial probability distribution over all possible hypotheses before any data is observed. Choosing an objective, universal prior that works in any environment is one of the foundational challenges in theoretical AI. Ray Solomonoff solved this problem in the 1960s by introducing a mathematical theory of inductive inference, now known as Solomonoff induction.

Kolmogorov Complexity and Occam's Razor

To assign prior probabilities to hypotheses, Solomonoff induction relies on Kolmogorov complexity. In this framework, environments and data-generating processes are treated as computer programs run on a universal Turing machine.

The Kolmogorov complexity of a piece of data (represented as a binary string) is the length of the shortest computer program that can output that exact string. This concept provides a rigorous mathematical definition for simplicity.

Solomonoff induction applies Occam's razor—the principle that the simplest explanation is usually the best—by favoring shorter programs. Because shorter programs have lower Kolmogorov complexity, they are considered simpler and are assigned a higher initial probability of being the true underlying cause of the observed data.

Algorithmic Probability

While Kolmogorov complexity only considers the single shortest program that produces an output, multiple different programs might generate the exact same data. To account for this, Solomonoff introduced Epicurus's principle of multiple explanations, which states that one should keep all hypotheses that are consistent with the observed data.

This leads to the concept of algorithmic probability. The algorithmic probability of a data string is the probability that a universal Turing machine will output that string when fed a randomly generated program (like flipping a coin to generate binary code).

Mathematically, a program of length \(L\) has a probability of \(2^{-L}\) of being generated randomly. Algorithmic probability sums the probabilities (\(2^{-L}\)) of all halting programs that produce the target data string. Because of the \(2^{-L}\) weighting, the shortest programs (those with the lowest Kolmogorov complexity) dominate the sum, naturally combining Occam's razor with Epicurus's principle.

Defining the Idealized Universal Prior

The universal prior, often called the Solomonoff prior and denoted as \(M(x)\), is defined directly by the algorithmic probability of a string \(x\). It serves as the ultimate prior distribution for an idealized AI agent because it considers every computable hypothesis simultaneously, weighted by its simplicity.

For an AI using Solomonoff induction, learning and prediction work through a straightforward application of Bayes' theorem:

  1. Prior Assignment: The AI assigns a prior probability to every possible computable environment based on the length of its code (\(2^{-L}\)).
  2. Observation: The AI observes a sequence of data from its environment.
  3. Elimination: Any program that cannot generate the observed data sequence is assigned a probability of zero and eliminated.
  4. Prediction: To predict the next piece of data, the AI uses the remaining valid programs. Each valid program makes a prediction, and the AI weighs these predictions based on the programs' initial algorithmic probabilities.

Incomputability and Role in AI

Solomonoff's universal prior is mathematically optimal. It is proven to converge on the true probability distribution of any computable environment faster than any other method, requiring minimal data to make accurate predictions. When combined with sequential decision theory, Solomonoff induction forms the basis for AIXI, the mathematical model of a universally optimal reinforcement learning agent.

However, the universal prior is an idealized concept because it is incomputable. Calculating Kolmogorov complexity and algorithmic probability requires knowing which programs will eventually halt and produce an output, and which will run forever in an infinite loop. Due to Alan Turing's Halting Problem, it is mathematically impossible to determine this for all programs in finite time.

Despite being impossible to implement directly, Solomonoff induction provides the gold standard for machine learning. Practical AI algorithms, such as Minimum Description Length (MDL) and modern compression-based neural networks, act as computable approximations of this idealized universal prior.
