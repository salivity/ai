Conversational Query Rewriting in Multi-Turn Search Dialogues

This article provides a quick overview of how Conversational Query Rewriting (CQR) resolves ellipses and pronouns in multi-turn search dialogues, enabling retrieval artificial intelligence to understand standalone user intent. In conversational search, users naturally omit previously mentioned words (ellipses) and rely on reference words like "it" or "they" (pronouns) as a dialogue progresses. Because traditional search retrievers process queries in isolation, these linguistic shortcuts confuse the AI and lead to poor search results. This article explores the mechanics of CQR, detailing how it uses conversation history to replace ambiguous pronouns, fill in missing context, and generate self-contained queries that retrieval systems can accurately process.

The Challenge of Multi-Turn Search

Traditional information retrieval systems are designed for single-turn interactions. A user types a complete thought, and the system fetches relevant documents. However, in a multi-turn conversational setting, humans rely on shared context to communicate efficiently. Instead of repeating the main subject in every question, users use two main linguistic shortcuts:

  • Pronouns (Anaphora): Words like "he," "it," "their," or "that" used to refer back to an entity mentioned in an earlier turn.
  • Ellipses: The omission of words or phrases that are implied by the previous context.

For example, consider this two-turn dialogue:

  • Turn 1: "When was the Eiffel Tower built?"
  • Turn 2: "How tall is it?" (Pronoun)
  • Turn 3: "And the Louvre?" (Ellipsis—omitting "When was it built and how tall is it?")

If a retrieval AI processes Turn 2 or Turn 3 as standalone queries, the search fails. Searching for "How tall is it?" or "And the Louvre?" yields irrelevant results because the AI lacks the context established in Turn 1.

How Conversational Query Rewriting Works

Conversational Query Rewriting bridges the gap between human conversation and machine retrieval. Acting as a middleware layer, a CQR model takes the current incomplete query and the historical dialogue context, then outputs a fully rewritten, context-independent query.

Large Language Models (LLMs) and specialized sequence-to-sequence transformers perform this task through three distinct steps:

1. Context Ingestion and Entity Tracking

The CQR model ingests the entire conversation history (or a sliding window of recent turns) alongside the latest user prompt. Using attention mechanisms, the model identifies and tracks key entities, subjects, and intents established in earlier turns. In the previous example, the model flags "Eiffel Tower" as the primary entity and "built date" and "height" as the active intents.

2. Coreference Resolution (Fixing Pronouns)

To resolve pronouns, the CQR model performs coreference resolution. It calculates the probability of which historical entity a current pronoun refers to. When the user asks, "How tall is it?", the model links the pronoun "it" to the noun phrase "the Eiffel Tower" from Turn 1. The model then substitutes the pronoun with the explicit entity, transforming the query into: "How tall is the Eiffel Tower?"

3. Ellipsis Resolution (Filling in the Blanks)

Resolving ellipses requires the model to recognize structural gaps in the user's latest input. When the user asks, "And the Louvre?", the CQR model detects that a subject is present ("the Louvre"), but the predicate (the action or question) is missing. The model looks back at the preceding turns to find the active predicate. It extracts the intent from the previous turns and applies it to the new entity, rewriting the query into: "How tall is the Louvre and when was it built?"

Empowering Retrieval AI with Standalone Intent

Once the CQR module resolves the pronouns and ellipses, it passes the rewritten query to the retrieval AI (such as a dense vector retriever or a traditional keyword-based BM25 engine).

Because the query now contains standalone intent, the retrieval system does not need to be redesigned to understand complex conversational states. It simply processes "How tall is the Eiffel Tower?" as a standard, single-turn search. This decoupled architecture allows developers to pair state-of-the-art conversational interfaces with fast, existing search indexes, ensuring high accuracy and relevant results throughout a multi-turn dialogue.
