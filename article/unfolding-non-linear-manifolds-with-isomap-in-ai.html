Unfolding Non-Linear Manifolds with Isomap in AI

Isometric Feature Mapping, commonly known as Isomap, is a foundational unsupervised machine learning algorithm designed for non-linear dimensionality reduction. This article explores how Isomap overcomes the limitations of traditional linear methods by constructing neighborhood graphs, calculating geodesic shortest-path distances, and applying Multidimensional Scaling to effectively unfold complex, high-dimensional manifolds into lower-dimensional representations without losing the intrinsic geometry of the data.

High-dimensional data in artificial intelligence often lies on a curved, lower-dimensional surface called a manifold. Traditional linear dimensionality reduction techniques, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), fail to capture these non-linear structures because they rely on straight-line Euclidean distances. In a curved manifold—frequently visualized as a "Swiss roll"—two data points might be very close in three-dimensional space based on Euclidean distance, yet remain far apart if you are forced to travel along the surface of the manifold itself. Isomap solves this problem by measuring the geodesic distance, which is the shortest path between two points strictly along the curved surface of the manifold.

To approximate this true geodesic distance without an explicit mathematical formula for the unknown manifold, Isomap breaks the process down into three distinct steps.

The first step is constructing a neighborhood graph. The algorithm evaluates every data point and connects it to its closest neighbors using standard Euclidean distance. This works under the assumption that a curved surface looks flat if you zoom in close enough; therefore, over very short distances, Euclidean distance is an accurate stand-in for geodesic distance. A neighborhood is defined either by connecting a point to its \(k\)-nearest neighbors (k-Isomap) or by connecting all points that fall within a fixed radius, epsilon (\(\epsilon\)-Isomap). The result is a weighted graph where the nodes are the data points and the edges represent the local Euclidean distances between immediate neighbors.

The second step is computing the shortest paths across the neighborhood graph. To find the distance between points that are not immediate neighbors, Isomap calculates the shortest path between all pairs of nodes by stepping solely along the established edges of the graph. Algorithms like Dijkstra’s algorithm or the Floyd-Warshall algorithm are utilized for this phase. By summing the local Euclidean edge weights along the shortest path between two distant nodes, Isomap accurately approximates the global geodesic distance. This step effectively severs the "short-circuit" Euclidean connections that cut through empty space, forcing the distance measurement to follow the true geometry of the manifold.

The third and final step is constructing the lower-dimensional embedding using Multidimensional Scaling (MDS). Once the shortest-path calculations are complete, the algorithm compiles a comprehensive matrix containing the geodesic distances between every possible pair of data points. Isomap feeds this geodesic distance matrix into classical MDS. MDS then calculates a new configuration of points in a lower-dimensional space (such as 2D or 3D) where the standard, straight-line Euclidean distances between the new points match the geodesic distances from the high-dimensional space as closely as possible.

Through this three-step process, Isomap successfully unfolds the non-linear manifold. By trusting Euclidean distances only at the local level and chaining them together via shortest-path algorithms to understand the global structure, Isomap preserves the intrinsic geometry of complex datasets. This allows unsupervised AI systems to visualize, cluster, and analyze intricate patterns that would otherwise remain hidden in high-dimensional space.
