Mapping Dialects with AI and Acoustic Forced Alignment

Sociolinguists are revolutionizing the study of regional dialects and vowel shifts by combining artificial intelligence with large-scale acoustic forced alignment to analyze oral history archives. Historically, mapping phonetic variation required manual transcription and acoustic measurement, a process that took hours for just a few minutes of audio. Today, automated computational pipelines allow researchers to process thousands of hours of archival recordings in a fraction of the time. By aligning transcripts with audio waves and extracting acoustic data via machine learning, linguists can track how regional accents evolve across decades and generations with unprecedented speed and accuracy.

Unlocking Oral History Archives

Oral history archives represent a goldmine for studying language change over time. Libraries, universities, and historical societies hold vast collections of interviews with speakers born as far back as the mid-19th century. These recordings capture authentic, unscripted vernacular speech. However, the sheer volume of these archives previously made large-scale phonetic analysis impossible. By digitizing these collections and applying AI-driven tools, sociolinguists can now conduct real-time and apparent-time studies of language evolution across entire populations rather than relying on small sample sizes.

The Role of Acoustic Forced Alignment

The foundational technology enabling this large-scale analysis is acoustic forced alignment. Forced alignment is an automated process that takes an orthographic transcript (the written words) and an audio file, and determines the exact millisecond each word and individual speech sound (phoneme) begins and ends.

To achieve this, tools like the Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA), FAVE (Forced Alignment and Vowel Extraction), and DARLA use pronunciation dictionaries to translate written words into phonetic transcriptions. Acoustic models—trained on vast amounts of speech data—then scan the audio waveform to match the expected phonetic sequences with the actual acoustic signals. The output is a time-aligned text grid that pinpoints the exact location of millions of individual vowels and consonants within an archive.

Extracting Vowel Formants

Once the forced aligner has isolated the exact boundaries of every vowel in a recording, the next step is measuring the acoustic properties of those vowels to map regional shifts. Vowels are defined acoustically by their formants, which are resonant frequencies of the vocal tract.

Linguists focus primarily on the first two formants:

  • Formant 1 (F1): Correlates inversely with vowel height (how high or low the tongue is in the mouth).
  • Formant 2 (F2): Correlates with vowel advancement (how far forward or back the tongue is).

Automated scripts extract the F1 and F2 frequencies at the midpoint of each aligned vowel. Because every person has a differently sized vocal tract, algorithms then normalize these measurements. Normalization allows researchers to accurately compare the vowel spaces of a tall adult male recorded in 1950 with a short young female recorded in 2020.

How AI Accelerates the Pipeline

Artificial intelligence and deep learning have drastically improved every stage of this sociolinguistic workflow. First, Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) models, such as OpenAI's Whisper or Kaldi-based systems, are used to generate initial transcripts for untranscribed oral histories, bypassing the bottleneck of manual transcription.

Second, deep neural networks are now integrated into forced aligners. Traditional aligners struggled with the degraded audio quality, background noise, and overlapping speech common in archival field recordings. Modern AI acoustic models can adapt to noisy environments and unfamiliar dialects, significantly reducing alignment errors.

Finally, machine learning algorithms are used to clean the extracted formant data. When processing millions of vowels, automated systems inevitably make some measurement errors. AI classifiers can automatically detect and filter out acoustic outliers, ensuring that the final dataset reflects true human speech patterns rather than software glitches.

Mapping Regional Vowel Shifts

With clean, normalized acoustic data extracted from thousands of speakers, sociolinguists use statistical modeling and geographic information systems (GIS) to map dialect variation. By plotting F1 and F2 values on a two-dimensional grid, researchers can visualize a speaker's vowel space and see how it shifts based on region, age, gender, and socioeconomic status.

This methodology has allowed linguists to track massive phonetic changes, such as the Northern Cities Vowel Shift in the Great Lakes region, the Southern Vowel Shift in the American South, and the Low-Back-Merger Shift in Canada and the Western United States. By linking acoustic AI pipelines with historical metadata, researchers can watch a vowel shift originate in a specific urban center and diffuse geographically across an archive's timeline, turning static historical recordings into dynamic maps of human language change.
