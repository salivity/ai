Decoding Hidden States with the Viterbi Algorithm

This article explores how the Viterbi algorithm efficiently identifies the most probable sequence of hidden states within sequential artificial intelligence models, such as Hidden Markov Models. By leveraging dynamic programming, the Markov property, and the principle of optimality, the algorithm avoids the impossible computational burden of brute-force calculations. Readers will understand the exact mathematical and structural mechanisms that allow the Viterbi algorithm to reduce exponential complexity to linear time, making tasks like speech recognition and natural language processing computationally feasible.

Sequential artificial intelligence models deal with observable events caused by underlying, unobservable factors known as hidden states. For example, in speech recognition, the audio signal is the observable event, while the actual words being spoken are the hidden states. Finding the single most likely sequence of these hidden states is known as decoding.

If a model has \(N\) possible hidden states and a sequence of length \(T\), there are \(N^T\) possible state sequences. Evaluating every possible path using a brute-force approach requires exponential time, which quickly becomes impossible as the sequence grows. The Viterbi algorithm solves this by reducing the time complexity from \(O(N^T)\) to \(O(T \times N^2)\).

This dramatic increase in efficiency relies on three core concepts:

1. The Markov Property Sequential models like Hidden Markov Models operate on the first-order Markov assumption. This states that the probability of transitioning to a future hidden state depends only on the current hidden state, not on the sequence of states that preceded it. Because the history prior to the current step does not alter future transition probabilities, the algorithm does not need to track every historical path simultaneously.

2. The Principle of Optimality The Viterbi algorithm is a dynamic programming method, meaning it breaks a complex problem down into simpler subproblems. It relies on the principle of optimality: if the most likely overall path passes through a specific state at a specific time step, then the path leading up to that state must also be the most likely path to that point.

3. Pruning Suboptimal Paths (The Trellis Structure) Instead of calculating full paths from start to finish, the Viterbi algorithm moves forward one time step at a time through a grid-like structure called a trellis.

At each time step \(t\), and for every possible hidden state, the algorithm looks at all the states from the previous time step \(t-1\). It calculates the probability of transitioning from each previous state to the current state, multiplied by the probability of the current state generating the observed data.

Here is where the efficiency happens: for each state at time \(t\), the algorithm only keeps the single highest-probability path leading into it and discards the rest. If there are 10 possible hidden states, the algorithm only ever maintains 10 active paths at any given time step, regardless of how long the sequence becomes.

Backtracking to Find the Sequence While moving forward through the sequence, the algorithm records "backpointers." A backpointer simply remembers which previous state at \(t-1\) produced the highest probability for the current state at \(t\).

Once the algorithm reaches the final time step \(T\), it selects the state with the highest final probability. It then follows the backpointers in reverse from the end of the sequence to the beginning. This reconstructs the exact sequence of hidden states that produced that maximum probability.

Preventing Numerical Underflow In addition to structural efficiency, the Viterbi algorithm is computationally stable. Finding the probability of a sequence requires multiplying many small probabilities (numbers between 0 and 1) together. Over a long sequence, this results in numbers so small that computer hardware rounds them to zero, a problem called underflow. The Viterbi algorithm converts probabilities into logarithms. This turns multiplication operations into simple addition operations, preserving accuracy and further speeding up processing time on modern processors.
