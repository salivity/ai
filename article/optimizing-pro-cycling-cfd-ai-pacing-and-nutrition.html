Optimizing Pro Cycling: CFD, AI Pacing, and Nutrition

Modern professional cycling has evolved into a high-tech science where marginal gains determine victory. This article explores how elite peloton teams combine Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) to achieve peak performance. By simulating airflows, teams perfect their time-trial drafting formations to minimize wind resistance. Simultaneously, AI algorithms analyze real-time power-meter data to establish optimal pacing strategies and calculate precise nutritional glycogen intake, ensuring riders maintain maximum output without exhausting their energy reserves prematurely.

Aerodynamic Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD)

Wind resistance is the single greatest obstacle a cyclist faces at high speeds. Professional teams use Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD)—advanced software that simulates airflow around 3D models of riders and their bicycles—to study and reduce aerodynamic drag before ever entering a physical wind tunnel.

In team time trials and peloton formations, CFD goes beyond analyzing the individual rider to evaluate how groups interact with the wind. Engineers input the exact body measurements and riding postures of an entire squad into the software. The simulations reveal how high-pressure air pockets form in front of the lead rider and how low-pressure wakes trail behind them.

Using this data, teams optimize their drafting formations in the following ways:

  • Rider Sequencing: CFD determines the exact order in which cyclists should ride based on their body sizes. Placing a broader rider in front of a smaller rider isn't always optimal; the software finds the smoothest transition of airflow from one rider's back to the next rider's chest to minimize turbulence.
  • The Push Effect: Simulations show that a rider drafting closely behind the leader actually reduces the aerodynamic drag of the lead rider by filling the low-pressure void behind them. CFD calculates the exact millimeter distance riders must maintain between wheels to maximize this mutual benefit.
  • Crosswind Echelons: Since wind rarely blows directly head-on, CFD models yaw angles (crosswinds) to prescribe the exact diagonal angle a team should form across the road, ensuring every rider remains shielded in the slipstream.

AI-Driven Power-Meter Pacing

While CFD minimizes the energy required to move through the air, AI ensures the energy a rider does use is distributed perfectly across a race. Every rider's bike is equipped with a power meter that measures their physical output in watts.

AI pacing platforms ingest historical rider physiology, course topography, real-time weather data, and the aerodynamic metrics gathered from CFD. During a time trial, the AI dictates a dynamic pacing strategy.

  • Terrain Optimization: Rather than maintaining a steady power output, the AI instructs riders to push harder on uphill sections and into headwinds, where aerodynamic drag is less penalizing, and to recover slightly on fast descents where pushing extra watts yields minimal speed gains.
  • Pull Duration: In a team formation, the AI calculates the exact number of seconds a rider should spend at the front of the line before rotating to the back. This prevents the lead rider from crossing their anaerobic threshold, ensuring they can recover fully while drafting in the slipstream.

Synchronizing Glycogen Intake

Physical pacing is useless if the athlete runs out of fuel. The human body stores a limited amount of carbohydrates as glycogen in the muscles and liver. Once depleted, a cyclist hits the "wall" (bonking), resulting in a catastrophic drop in power.

The same AI systems that monitor power output also track glycogen depletion in real time. Because mechanical work (measured in kilojoules by the power meter) correlates directly to caloric expenditure, the AI knows precisely how much energy a rider is burning second by second.

By combining power data with heart rate and core body temperature sensors, the AI estimates the exact ratio of fat versus carbohydrates (glycogen) the rider is metabolizing at any given moment. The system then alerts both the team car and the rider’s handlebar computer when it is time to consume carbohydrates.

Instead of eating on a generic schedule, riders are prompted to ingest specific amounts of glucose-fructose gels or liquids—often up to 120 grams of carbohydrates per hour—tailored to their exact metabolic burn rate. This ensures their glycogen stores remain topped up for the decisive final kilometers of the race without causing gastrointestinal distress from overfeeding.
