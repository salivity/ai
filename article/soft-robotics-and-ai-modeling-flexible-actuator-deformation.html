Soft Robotics and AI: Modeling Flexible Actuator Deformation

This article provides a direct look into the field of soft robotics and explains how artificial intelligence (AI) solves its most complex engineering hurdle: controlling flexible movement. You will learn the fundamental definition of soft robotics, why the non-linear deformation of flexible actuators challenges traditional mathematical modeling, and the exact machine learning methods—such as neural networks and reinforcement learning—used to predict and control these adaptable machines in real time.

What is Soft Robotics?

Soft robotics is a subfield of robotics that designs and builds machines from highly compliant, flexible materials rather than rigid metals and hard plastics. Inspired by biological organisms like octopuses, worms, and human muscle tissue, soft robots use materials such as silicone, elastomers, hydrogels, and flexible fabrics.

Because they lack rigid skeletons, soft robots can squeeze through tight spaces, safely interact with delicate objects, and adapt their shape to unpredictable environments. This makes them ideal for minimally invasive surgery, handling fragile agricultural produce, and wearable rehabilitation devices.

The Challenge of Non-Linear Deformation

Soft robots move using flexible actuators, which are components that change shape when driven by air pressure (pneumatics), fluid pressure (hydraulics), heat, or electrical currents. While this flexibility is a soft robot's greatest strength, it is also its biggest control challenge.

In a traditional rigid robot, movement is linear and predictable. Moving a metal joint by a specific angle puts the robotic arm in an exact, easily calculated position. Soft actuators, however, experience complex non-linear deformation. This means:

  • Infinite Degrees of Freedom: A soft actuator can bend, twist, stretch, and compress at any point along its body simultaneously.
  • Material Hysteresis: Soft materials do not always return to their exact original shape at the same rate they deformed. Their current state depends heavily on their past movements.
  • Viscoelasticity: The materials exhibit both fluid-like and solid-like characteristics, meaning their stiffness and response change depending on how fast or hard force is applied.

Because of these factors, traditional physics-based equations (like Finite Element Modeling) require immense computational power and are too slow to control a soft robot in real time.

How AI Models Flexible Actuators

Artificial intelligence bypasses the need to manually calculate complex physics equations. Instead of relying on rigid mathematical formulas, AI uses data-driven approaches to learn how a flexible actuator behaves.

1. Deep Neural Networks for Kinematics Engineers embed flexible sensors inside the soft actuator and record thousands of movements using motion-capture cameras. Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) analyze this dataset, mapping the relationship between the input (e.g., how much air pressure is pumped into the actuator) and the output (the resulting 3D shape and position). Once trained, the neural network can instantly predict how the actuator will deform under new inputs.

2. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) for Material Memory Because soft materials suffer from hysteresis—meaning their current shape is influenced by their previous shapes—standard AI models aren't always enough. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are used because they possess internal "memory." They process sequences of data over time, allowing the AI to accurately predict deformation based on what the actuator was doing milliseconds or seconds ago.

3. Reinforcement Learning for Autonomous Control In Reinforcement Learning (RL), the AI controls the soft actuator through trial and error. The AI is given a goal, such as "grip the object" or "move forward," and is rewarded when its actions bring the actuator closer to that goal. Over thousands of simulated or real-world attempts, the AI teaches itself the exact combinations of pressure and force needed to navigate the non-linear deformations, without a human ever programming the physics of the material.

4. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) To reduce the massive amount of real-world data needed to train AI, researchers use Physics-Informed Neural Networks. PINNs combine machine learning with known laws of material science. The basic rules of elasticity and fluid dynamics are baked into the AI's learning algorithm. This prevents the AI from making physically impossible predictions and allows it to model complex actuator deformations faster and with much less training data.
