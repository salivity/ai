Multimodal AI and Shared Latent Spaces

This article explores multimodal artificial intelligence models and the mechanisms they use to unify text, audio, and visual data. It begins by defining multimodal systems and contrasting them with traditional single-modality models. Next, it breaks down how specialized encoders convert raw inputs into mathematical vectors. Finally, it explains how these vectors are mapped and aligned within a shared latent space using techniques like contrastive learning and cross-modal attention to enable comprehensive machine understanding.

What are Multimodal AI Models?

Traditional artificial intelligence models are unimodal, meaning they are designed to process only one type of data, such as a text-only language model or an image-only classifier. Multimodal AI models, by contrast, are architectures capable of simultaneously processing, understanding, and generating information across multiple distinct data types—primarily text, audio, and visuals (images and video). By integrating these different sensory inputs, multimodal AI mimics human perception, allowing a system to hear a sound, see an object, and read a description while recognizing that they all refer to the same underlying concept.

Converting Data into Embeddings

Before a model can combine text, audio, and visuals, it must translate each raw data type into a uniform mathematical format known as an embedding (a high-dimensional vector of numbers). Because each data type has a completely different structure, multimodal models use modality-specific encoders to process them:

  • Text: Tokenizers break words into sub-word units, which are then processed by Transformer architectures to capture semantic meaning.
  • Visuals: Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) or Vision Transformers (ViTs) divide images into patches, analyzing pixel values, spatial relationships, and features.
  • Audio: Raw sound waves are typically converted into spectrograms (visual representations of frequencies over time) and processed using audio-specific Transformers to capture phonetic and acoustic patterns.

The Concept of a Shared Latent Space

Once the encoders convert raw inputs into embeddings, these vectors exist in separate mathematical spaces. A text vector and an image vector cannot yet be directly compared. To bridge this gap, the model uses projection layers to map these disparate embeddings into a shared latent space (or joint embedding space).

A shared latent space is a unified, multi-dimensional mathematical map where points close to one another represent similar semantic concepts, regardless of the original data format. In a properly aligned latent space, the text vector for the word "dog," the image vector of a golden retriever, and the audio vector of a bark will all cluster tightly together in the exact same geometric region.

How Alignment is Achieved

Getting these diverse embeddings to cluster correctly in the shared latent space requires training the model to align them. AI researchers primarily use three methods to achieve this alignment:

1. Contrastive Learning Contrastive learning trains the model using massive datasets of paired data (such as images and their corresponding text captions). The model's objective is to pull the embeddings of matching pairs closer together in the latent space while pushing non-matching pairs farther apart. By calculating the cosine similarity (the angle between vectors) across millions of examples, the model learns to map a picture of a sunset and the word "sunset" to the exact same coordinates.

2. Cross-Modal Attention Inside Transformer-based multimodal models, cross-attention mechanisms allow different modalities to directly reference one another. During processing, the model uses features from one modality (like text) to query features in another modality (like an image). This forces the model to align specific words with specific regions of an image or specific timestamps in an audio track, fusing the representations at a granular level.

3. Joint Reconstruction (Masked Modeling) In this approach, the model is given incomplete multimodal data—for example, a video with muted audio and missing words in the transcript. The model must use the remaining visual data to predict and reconstruct the missing audio and text. To succeed at this task, the internal representations must be deeply aligned within the shared latent space, forcing the model to learn the intrinsic relationships between sight, sound, and language.
