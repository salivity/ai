AI and Laser Tech in Robotic Shotcrete Tunnel Lining

Modern underground construction relies heavily on robotic shotcrete sprayers to quickly stabilize newly excavated caverns. This article explores how these advanced machines integrate 3D laser-scanned tunnel profiles with rebound-minimization artificial intelligence to automate the concrete lining process. By combining spatial mapping with machine learning, these robotic systems optimize spray trajectories, reduce material waste, and ensure structurally sound tunnel linings without putting human operators at risk.

Laser-Scanned Tunnel Profiling

Before a single drop of concrete is sprayed, the robotic system must understand the exact geometry of the newly excavated rock face. High-definition 3D LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) scanners mounted directly on the rig capture millions of data points across the cavern walls and ceiling.

This data is instantly compiled into a detailed digital twin of the tunnel. The onboard computer compares this "as-built" rock profile against the "as-designed" engineering blueprint. By calculating the difference between the jagged rock and the desired final thickness, the system generates a precise topological heat map. This map dictates exactly how much shotcrete is needed in every square inch of the cavern, identifying deep crevices that require extra filling and protruding rocks that need a thinner coat.

Rebound-Minimization Artificial Intelligence

A major challenge in shotcreting is "rebound"—the phenomenon where high-velocity concrete bounces off the hard rock wall instead of sticking to it. In manual operations, rebound can waste up to 30% of the concrete mix, driving up costs and creating hazardous cleanup conditions.

Rebound-minimization AI solves this by taking control of the spraying nozzle. Using real-time sensors, the AI constantly monitors and adjusts four critical variables:

  • Nozzle Angle: Concrete adheres best when sprayed at a strict 90-degree angle to the receiving surface. Because excavated rock is irregular, the AI uses the laser profile to articulate the robotic arm continuously, maintaining perpendiculatiry over bumps and fissures.
  • Standoff Distance: Spraying from too far away causes the concrete to lose velocity and drop; spraying too close forces the material to blast off the wall. The AI keeps the nozzle at the optimal distance—typically between 1 and 2 meters—regardless of the cavern's changing geometry.
  • Accelerator Dosing: Shotcrete requires chemical accelerators to solidify instantly upon impact. The AI calculates the exact chemical-to-concrete ratio needed in real-time based on the ambient temperature, humidity, and whether the concrete is being sprayed on a vertical wall or an overhead ceiling.
  • Pumping Pressure: The AI regulates the pneumatic pressure to match the viscosity of the current concrete batch, ensuring a smooth, uninterrupted flow that minimizes splatter.

Automated Execution and Real-Time Feedback

When the spraying sequence begins, the robotic arm follows a pre-calculated path generated from the initial laser scan. However, the system does not run blindly. As layers of shotcrete build up, the LiDAR scanners continue to sweep the wall, providing live feedback to the AI.

If the sensors detect that a specific section is accumulating concrete slower than expected due to rock slickness or groundwater seepage, the AI adapts on the fly. It slows the robotic arm's movement and adjusts the chemical accelerator to force adherence. Once the sensors confirm the lining has reached the exact millimeter thickness required by the engineering blueprint, the robot automatically moves to the next section. This closed-loop system guarantees a uniform structural shell, reduces material waste to less than 10%, and drastically accelerates the timeline of underground cavern excavation.
