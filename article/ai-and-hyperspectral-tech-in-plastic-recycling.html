AI and Hyperspectral Tech in Plastic Recycling

Modern recycling facilities are revolutionizing waste management by combining hyperspectral imaging and artificial intelligence (AI) robotics to accurately sort plastics by their specific polymer types. Traditional sorting methods often fail to distinguish between look-alike plastics, leading to cross-contamination and downgraded recycled materials. By deploying hyperspectral cameras that read the unique chemical signatures of plastics alongside high-speed AI-driven robots that physically separate the items, facilities can now achieve unprecedented purity levels, making plastic recycling significantly more efficient and economically viable.

The Role of Hyperspectral Imaging

Standard optical cameras only see colors in the visible light spectrum, making it impossible to tell the difference between a clear polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottle and a clear polyvinyl chloride (PVC) bottle. Hyperspectral cameras solve this problem by operating in the near-infrared (NIR) spectrum.

Here is how the imaging process works on the conveyor belt:

  • Illumination: As mixed plastics move rapidly along a conveyor belt, they are illuminated by powerful halogen or LED lights.
  • Spectral Reflection: Different plastic polymers absorb and reflect this light in unique ways based on their molecular structure.
  • Chemical Fingerprinting: The hyperspectral camera captures the reflected NIR light across hundreds of narrow wavelength bands. Because the carbon-hydrogen bonds in plastics vibrate differently depending on the polymer, the camera reads a unique "chemical fingerprint" for each item.
  • Polymer Identification: Within milliseconds, the system identifies the exact polymer—such as PET, HDPE (high-density polyethylene), PP (polypropylene), or PS (polystyrene)—regardless of the plastic's shape, dirtiness, or surface color.

How AI Processes the Data

While hyperspectral cameras identify the material composition, artificial intelligence provides the visual and spatial context needed for physical sorting.

Standard optical sensors work alongside the hyperspectral cameras to feed data into deep learning algorithms, typically Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). The AI merges the chemical data from the hyperspectral sensor with visual data regarding the object's size, shape, and orientation.

The AI uses this combined data to:

  • Determine the exact center of gravity of the plastic item for optimal gripping.
  • Differentiate between overlapping items on a crowded conveyor belt.
  • Distinguish between food-grade and non-food-grade plastics of the same polymer type based on packaging recognition.
  • Continuously learn and adapt to new packaging designs introduced into the waste stream.

High-Speed Robotic Separation

Once the hyperspectral camera identifies the polymer and the AI maps its location, the data is transmitted to the physical sorting mechanism. Modern facilities use two primary methods triggered by this data: pneumatic air jets and robotic arms.

For robotic sorting, facilities predominantly use delta robots—spider-like robotic arms suspended above the conveyor belt.

  1. Tracking: The AI calculates the speed of the conveyor belt and predicts where the target plastic will be in a fraction of a second.
  2. Extraction: The robotic arm swoops down and uses a vacuum suction cup or a mechanical gripper to pick up the specific plastic item.
  3. Sorting: The robot places the item into a designated chute for that specific polymer type (e.g., placing an HDPE milk jug into the HDPE bin).
  4. Speed: These AI-driven robots can perform up to 70 to 80 picks per minute, operating 24/7 with an accuracy rate exceeding 95%, far surpassing human capabilities.

By integrating hyperspectral vision with AI robotics, recycling facilities effectively eliminate human error, drastically reduce polymer cross-contamination, and produce high-purity recycled resin that can directly compete with virgin plastics.
