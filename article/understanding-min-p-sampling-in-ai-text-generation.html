Understanding Min-P Sampling in AI Text Generation

This article explores the mechanics of Min-p sampling in autoregressive artificial intelligence generation. It explains how this decoding method dynamically adjusts the minimum probability threshold for next-token selection based on the model's confidence in the highest-scoring token. By examining the mathematical formula, practical examples, and comparisons with traditional sampling techniques, this overview demonstrates how Min-p sampling balances creativity and coherence in large language models.

The Core Mechanism of Min-P Sampling

In autoregressive language models, text is generated one token at a time. For each step, the model calculates a probability distribution across its entire vocabulary. Sampling methods filter this distribution to prevent the model from choosing nonsensical words while still allowing for varied outputs.

Min-p sampling achieves this by setting a dynamic threshold based on the top token's probability (\(P_{max}\)). Instead of using a hard cutoff or accumulating probabilities, a base value (\(p_{base}\)) is chosen by the user, typically between 0.01 and 0.2. The actual minimum threshold (\(P_{threshold}\)) for any given step is calculated using a simple multiplication:

\(P_{threshold} = p_{base} \times P_{max}\)

Any token with a probability lower than \(P_{threshold}\) is discarded. The remaining tokens have their probabilities normalized, and the next token is sampled from this reduced pool.

Scaling Relative to Top Token Confidence

The defining feature of Min-p sampling is its responsiveness to the model's certainty. Because the threshold scales directly with \(P_{max}\), the filter adapts to the context of the generation step.

High Confidence Scenarios

When the model is highly confident about the next word—for instance, completing a common idiom or writing code syntax—the top token will have a very high probability.

  • Top token probability (\(P_{max}\)): 0.90 (90%)
  • Base Min-p setting (\(p_{base}\)): 0.10 (10%)
  • Resulting Threshold: \(0.90 \times 0.10 = 0.09\) (9%)

In this scenario, any token with a probability below 9% is eliminated. Since the top token already takes up 90% of the probability mass, almost all other tokens will fall below the 9% mark. The model is forced to pick the obvious, correct token, preventing hallucinations or errors.

Low Confidence Scenarios

When the model is at a creative crossroads—such as starting a new sentence in a fictional story—the probability distribution flattens out, and the top token has a much lower probability.

  • Top token probability (\(P_{max}\)): 0.15 (15%)
  • Base Min-p setting (\(p_{base}\)): 0.10 (10%)
  • Resulting Threshold: \(0.15 \times 0.10 = 0.015\) (1.5%)

Here, the threshold drops significantly to 1.5%. This allows a much wider variety of tokens to remain in the pool, encouraging diverse and creative outputs when there is no single "correct" next word.

Why Relative Scaling Matters

Traditional sampling methods like Top-p (nucleus sampling) and Top-k struggle to adapt to shifting contexts.

Top-k always selects a fixed number of tokens. If \(k=50\), it forces 50 options into the pool even when only one word makes grammatical sense.

Top-p sums the probabilities of the top tokens until a threshold (like 0.90) is reached. If the top token has a probability of 0.89, Top-p still has to include additional low-probability tokens to reach the 0.90 mark, which introduces a small risk of selecting an irrelevant word. Conversely, in low-confidence scenarios, Top-p might include hundreds of extremely low-probability tokens, leading to incoherent text at higher temperatures.

Min-p bypasses these issues by making the cutoff strictly relative to the best option available. It permits high temperatures (which increase creativity by flattening probability distributions) without allowing the tail-end of garbage tokens to ruin the output, as the threshold naturally scales to block tokens that are disproportionately unlikely compared to the top choice.
