How AI Speaker Diarization Segments Multi-Person Audio

Speaker diarization is an artificial intelligence process that partitions an audio stream involving multiple people into homogeneous segments based on the identity of each speaker. Answering the fundamental question of "who spoke when," this technology relies on a multi-stage pipeline—including voice activity detection, audio segmentation, feature extraction, and clustering—to accurately separate and label individual voices without needing prior knowledge of the speakers. This article breaks down the exact step-by-step mechanisms AI systems use to isolate, analyze, and group human voices from complex, multi-person recordings.

1. Voice Activity Detection (VAD)

The first step in speaker diarization is removing non-speech elements from the audio file. Voice Activity Detection (VAD) algorithms scan the recording to distinguish human speech from background noise, music, coughing, and silence. The AI strips away these non-speech sections and passes only the active voice data to the next stage of the pipeline. This reduces computational load and prevents background noise from being mistakenly identified as a unique speaker.

2. Audio Segmentation and Speaker Change Detection

Once the system isolates the speech, it must break the continuous audio stream into smaller, manageable pieces. This happens in one of two ways:

  • Uniform Segmentation: The AI slices the audio into fixed-length, overlapping windows (typically 1 to 2 seconds long). This ensures that each small segment likely contains the voice of only one person.
  • Speaker Change Detection: The AI actively listens for acoustic shifts—such as sudden changes in pitch, cadence, or tone—that indicate one person has stopped talking and another has begun. It then places a boundary marker at these exact transition points.

3. Feature Extraction and Speaker Embeddings

With the audio divided into short segments, the AI must analyze the vocal characteristics of each piece. Deep neural networks process the audio segments to extract acoustic features, such as pitch, timbre, and frequency modulation.

Modern AI systems convert these vocal features into fixed-length numerical vectors known as speaker embeddings (commonly d-vectors or x-vectors). These embeddings act as a digital voiceprint. Regardless of what words are being spoken, the mathematical representation of a specific person's voice will remain consistent across different segments, allowing the AI to map each voice to a multi-dimensional space.

4. Clustering

After generating a numerical embedding for every audio segment, the AI must figure out which segments belong to the same person. It achieves this through clustering algorithms, such as Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering (AHC) or Spectral Clustering.

The algorithm measures the mathematical distance (usually via cosine similarity) between all the embeddings. Embeddings that are closely grouped together in the vector space are merged into a single cluster. Each final cluster represents one distinct speaker. Through this method, the system also deduces the total number of speakers in the recording.

5. Resegmentation and Post-Processing

The initial segmentation and clustering processes can leave rough edges at the exact millisecond a speaker changes. During resegmentation, the AI uses statistical models, such as Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), to refine the boundaries between speakers. It smooths out the timeline and assigns final timestamps to each speaker cluster (e.g., "Speaker A: 0:00–0:14, Speaker B: 0:15–0:28").

End-to-End Neural Diarization (EEND)

Traditional pipelines process the steps above separately, which makes it difficult for the AI to handle overlapping speech—moments when two or more people talk at the exact same time.

To solve this, modern systems increasingly use End-to-End Neural Diarization (EEND). Instead of using a multi-step pipeline, EEND uses a single deep learning model trained to take raw audio as an input and output the speaker timestamps directly. Because the neural network processes the entire context of the audio simultaneously, it can accurately identify and label multiple speakers even when their voices overlap.
