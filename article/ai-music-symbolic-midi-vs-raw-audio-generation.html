AI Music: Symbolic MIDI vs. Raw Audio Generation

This article provides a clear examination of the structural differences between generating symbolic MIDI music scores and synthesizing raw audio waveforms using artificial intelligence. While symbolic generation operates on an abstract level to dictate musical events like pitch, rhythm, and tempo, raw audio synthesis constructs actual sound waves sample by sample. Understanding these two distinct approaches reveals how AI models process data density, handle computational requirements, and utilize different neural network architectures to create music.

Data Representation and Density

The primary structural difference between the two methods lies in the nature of the data being generated.

Symbolic MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) generation does not produce actual sound. Instead, it generates a digital music score composed of discrete events. The AI outputs instructions such as "note on," "note off," pitch values, velocity (loudness), and instrument assignments. Because a single second of music might only contain a few notes, the data is extremely lightweight and sparse. In this paradigm, the AI acts as a composer writing sheet music, which then requires a separate software synthesizer to actually produce sound.

Raw audio synthesis, conversely, generates the physical sound wave itself. Audio is typically sampled at rates between 16,000 and 44,100 times per second (Hz). Consequently, a raw audio AI must generate tens of thousands of continuous data points for just one second of music. In this paradigm, the AI acts as the composer, the musician, and the recording studio simultaneously, capturing acoustic nuances like timbre, vocal breath, and room reverb directly into the waveform.

Temporal Resolution and Context

Because of the massive difference in data density, the two approaches handle time and musical structure very differently.

Symbolic AI models easily grasp long-term musical structures. Because a three-minute song in MIDI format might only consist of a few thousand data tokens, an AI can easily hold the entire song in its memory. This allows the model to maintain a consistent melody, repeat a chorus accurately, and resolve chord progressions over several minutes.

Raw audio models struggle with long-term coherence due to the sheer volume of data. Generating a three-minute song at 44.1 kHz requires the AI to predict nearly eight million individual data points in sequence. While raw audio AI excels at micro-level textures—such as the realistic strumming of a guitar or the gravel in a singer's voice—it often loses track of the overarching melody or rhythm after a few seconds unless guided by complex, hierarchical conditioning systems.

Neural Network Architectures

To accommodate these structural differences, developers use distinct AI architectures for each task.

Symbolic music generation relies heavily on sequence-to-sequence models, predominantly Transformers—the same architecture used in large language models. Since MIDI data is essentially a vocabulary of musical notes and timings, the AI treats music like a language. It predicts the next musical note in a sequence based on the context of the previous notes.

Raw audio synthesis requires architectures capable of handling high-dimensional, continuous signals. Early models used autoregressive networks (like WaveNet) to predict one audio sample at a time, which was accurate but exceptionally slow. Modern raw audio AI utilizes Diffusion Models, Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). Often, these systems convert raw audio into spectrograms (visual representations of frequencies over time), generate the audio visually, and then use a neural vocoder to translate the image back into an audible waveform.

Computational Requirements

The computational gulf between symbolic and raw audio AI is vast. Symbolic MIDI models can be trained on consumer-grade GPUs and can generate a full musical score in milliseconds on a standard laptop CPU. Raw audio models require massive enterprise-level GPU clusters to train on millions of hours of audio data, and generating a single three-minute audio track still requires significant processing power and time.

Control and Editability

Finally, the output structure dictates how humans can interact with the AI's creation. Symbolic outputs are entirely editable. A human producer can open an AI-generated MIDI file in a Digital Audio Workstation (DAW), fix a wrong note, change the tempo, or swap a piano sound for a violin without losing quality. Raw audio outputs are "baked in." Like a finalized MP3 file, you cannot easily change a single note in a chord or swap out an instrument without regenerating the entire audio waveform from scratch.
