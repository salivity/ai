AI Slow Motion: How Optical Flow Frame Interpolation Works

This article explores how artificial intelligence transforms standard-frame-rate videos into ultra-smooth slow motion through optical-flow-guided frame interpolation. By analyzing pixel movement between sequential frames, these AI models calculate motion trajectories and synthesize entirely new intermediate frames. We will break down the core mechanics of optical flow, how neural networks warp pixels to generate nonexistent frames, and the ways AI overcomes complex visual challenges like motion blur and occlusions to produce seamless results.

Understanding Frame Interpolation

Standard videos are typically recorded at 24, 30, or 60 frames per second (fps). Slowing down a 30 fps video by half results in a choppy 15 fps playback because there is not enough visual data to fill the time gaps. Frame interpolation solves this by generating entirely new frames and inserting them between the existing ones. Instead of simply duplicating frames or blending them together—which causes a ghosting effect—AI models actually predict what the scene would look like at the exact fraction of a second between the recorded frames.

The Role of Optical Flow

Optical flow is the foundation of modern AI frame interpolation. It is a computer vision technique that calculates the apparent motion of individual pixels from one frame to the next.

When an AI model examines Frame A and Frame B, it generates an optical flow map. This map is a grid of vectors—arrows with both direction and magnitude—that tells the computer where every single pixel in Frame A moved to end up in Frame B. For example, if a car drives across the screen, the pixels making up the car will have high-magnitude vectors pointing in the direction of travel, while the stationary background pixels will have vectors of zero.

Estimating Intermediate Motion

Once the AI knows the start point (Frame A) and the end point (Frame B) of every pixel, it must calculate where those pixels should be at a specific point in time between the two frames.

To create a frame halfway between Frame A and Frame B, the AI divides the optical flow vectors in half. Advanced AI models use bidirectional optical flow, meaning they calculate the movement from A to B and from B to A simultaneously. This two-way analysis provides a much more accurate estimation of the motion trajectory, especially for objects that are accelerating, decelerating, or changing direction.

Warping and Frame Synthesis

With the intermediate motion vectors calculated, the model moves to the synthesis phase. A dedicated neural network takes the original pixels from Frame A and Frame B and "warps" them along the calculated flow vectors toward the middle timeframe.

Essentially, the AI pushes the pixels from the first frame forward in time and pulls the pixels from the second frame backward in time. These two warped images are then fused together to create the new, synthetic middle frame. Because the pixels are physically relocated rather than just faded together, the resulting image maintains sharp edges and realistic details.

Handling Occlusions and Complex Motion

The hardest part of frame interpolation is dealing with occlusions—areas of a scene that are visible in one frame but hidden in the next. For example, as a person walks in front of a signpost, the background pixels of the sign disappear in one frame and reappear in the next. Simple pixel warping leaves blank holes or bizarre stretching artifacts in these areas.

Optical-flow-guided AI models overcome this using deep learning refinement networks, often based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) or Vision Transformers. These networks are trained on thousands of hours of high-frame-rate video to understand object permanence and visual context.

When the optical flow process leaves a gap due to an occlusion, the synthesis network generates an occlusion mask. This mask tells the AI to pull pixel data exclusively from the frame where the background is actually visible, rather than blending both frames. If data is missing from both frames due to rapid motion blur, the AI hallucinates and fills in the missing textures based on the surrounding pixels.

The Final Output

By repeating this process—estimating flow, warping pixels, and refining occlusions—the AI can insert one, three, or even seven new frames between every original pair. This seamlessly scales a standard 30 fps video up to 240 fps, allowing editors to stretch the timeline into dramatic, fluid slow motion without needing specialized high-speed cameras.
