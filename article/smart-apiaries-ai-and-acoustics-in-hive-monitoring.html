Smart Apiaries: AI and Acoustics in Hive Monitoring

Smart apiaries are revolutionizing modern beekeeping by combining in-hive acoustic monitoring with edge artificial intelligence to safeguard honeybee colonies. By capturing the vibrations and sounds produced by bees, converting this audio into visual spectrograms, and analyzing the data directly at the hive using compact AI models, these systems can accurately detect critical events. Beekeepers receive real-time alerts regarding queen loss, impending swarming, and Varroa mite stress, allowing for timely interventions without the need for invasive manual inspections.

To understand the colony, smart hives utilize tiny, durable microphones and piezoelectric accelerometers placed directly inside the brood chamber. Bees communicate extensively through sound and vibration, generating specific frequencies by vibrating their wing muscles. Because raw audio files are difficult for standard algorithms to interpret directly, the system converts these acoustic recordings into spectrograms. A spectrogram is a visual representation of the spectrum of frequencies of a signal as it varies with time. Using Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithms, the hive’s audio is mapped into a two-dimensional image displaying time on the horizontal axis, frequency on the vertical axis, and amplitude as color intensity.

Once the audio is converted into a spectrogram image, edge artificial intelligence takes over. Instead of streaming massive audio files to the cloud—which requires high bandwidth and significant battery power—smart apiaries use edge AI. Small, low-power microcontrollers placed directly on the hive run lightweight Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). These neural networks are trained on thousands of hours of labeled bee sounds to recognize visual patterns within the spectrograms. When the edge AI identifies a specific acoustic signature, it transmits a tiny, low-data alert to the beekeeper's smartphone via cellular or LoRaWAN networks.

Detecting queen loss is one of the primary functions of this technology. A healthy, queen-right colony produces a steady, cohesive hum typically centered around 200 to 250 Hz. If the queen dies or goes missing, the colony quickly realizes the absence of her pheromones. Within hours, the bees become agitated, and their collective sound shifts into a louder, more chaotic "roar" that spikes into higher frequencies between 300 and 400 Hz. The edge AI recognizes this sustained upward shift in the spectrogram and immediately notifies the beekeeper that the hive is queenless.

Swarming, the natural process where a large portion of the colony leaves with the old queen to form a new hive, can severely deplete honey production. Fortunately, bees exhibit distinct acoustic behaviors days before a swarm occurs. The overall amplitude shifts rapidly in the 250 to 300 Hz band as worker bees prepare for flight. Furthermore, newly emerging or rival queens emit a very specific, high-pitched sound known as "piping," which comes in the form of "tooting" and "quacking" at frequencies between 350 and 550 Hz. The AI’s image recognition easily spots these distinct, narrow-band horizontal lines on the spectrogram, giving the beekeeper a crucial window to split the hive or add space before the bees depart.

Varroa destructor mites are the leading cause of colony collapse, and their presence also alters the acoustic profile of a hive. While mites themselves do not make detectable noises, the physiological stress and viruses they transmit heavily impact the bees. Infested bees exhibit altered wing-beat frequencies due to conditions like Deformed Wing Virus, and the colony engages in increased grooming and erratic movement to rid themselves of the parasites. Additionally, a mite-stressed colony struggles to maintain optimal brood nest temperatures, leading to abnormal ventilation fanning sounds. The edge AI detects these subtle, erratic deviations in the baseline acoustic spectrogram, alerting the beekeeper to a rising Varroa infestation long before the colony reaches a point of collapse.
