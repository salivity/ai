Trade Secrets vs. Fair Trials: AI Source Code in Court

When prosecutors rely on artificial intelligence to convict a defendant, a major legal conflict arises between corporate intellectual property rights and constitutional law. Software companies fiercely guard their source code as proprietary trade secrets to maintain their competitive edge in the market. Conversely, criminal defendants have a constitutional right to a fair trial, which includes inspecting and challenging the evidence used against them. This article explores how courts navigate this complex dilemma during the pre-trial discovery phase by requiring demonstrations of relevance, utilizing protective orders, and imposing severe evidentiary penalties when companies refuse to comply.

To access proprietary AI source code, a defendant cannot simply demand it; they must first establish materiality and relevance. Courts require the defense to show that inspecting the code is necessary to ensure a fair trial. Because AI tools—such as probabilistic genotyping software, facial recognition, and predictive policing algorithms—can contain human bias, coding errors, or flawed training data, defendants argue that the software acts as a digital witness. Under the Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause and the Due Process Clause, defendants have the right to cross-examine witnesses and inspect critical evidence. If the defense proves that the AI’s output is central to the prosecution's case and that the software's reliability cannot be verified without looking under the hood, the court will generally favor the defendant's constitutional rights over the company's financial interests.

On the other side of the issue, technology vendors argue that disclosing their source code in court could destroy their businesses. Developing forensic AI requires millions of dollars in research and development. Software companies assert trade-secret privileges, warning that if their proprietary algorithms are exposed during public litigation, competitors could steal their work, rendering the company bankrupt. Furthermore, vendors often argue that publicizing the code could allow bad actors to learn how to manipulate or evade the AI system in the future.

Courts balance these competing interests primarily through the use of strict protective orders and in camera (private) judicial reviews. Rather than ordering a company to hand over its code to the public, a judge will establish secure conditions for the discovery process. A standard protective order in these cases typically includes the following stipulations:

  • Expert-Only Access: Only qualified, court-approved defense experts are allowed to view the source code.
  • Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs): The defense team and their experts must sign legally binding NDAs that carry heavy penalties for leaks.
  • Secure Viewing Rooms: The code must be inspected on a standalone computer not connected to the internet, often at the software company’s own facility.
  • No Copying: Defense experts are prohibited from copying, printing, or removing any lines of code from the secure environment.
  • Sealed Court Records: Any subsequent court filings or testimony that specifically detail the proprietary code are sealed from the public record.

Ultimately, if a judge orders the disclosure of the source code under a protective order and the software company still refuses to comply, the court prioritizes the constitution. Because the court cannot force a private third-party vendor in a criminal case to hand over property if they choose to withdraw, the penalty falls on the prosecution. In these instances, the judge will rule the AI-generated evidence inadmissible. The prosecution must then proceed to trial without the AI evidence, which ensures that a person's liberty is never sacrificed to protect a corporate secret.
