AI and Laser Sorting in Construction Demolition Recycling

Construction and demolition recyclers are increasingly turning to advanced optical sorting technologies to purify crushed waste streams. By combining laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) with artificial intelligence and high-speed pneumatic ejection, recycling facilities can accurately distinguish and separate autoclaved aerated concrete (AAC), gypsum drywall, and clean brick aggregate. This automated approach ensures high-purity recycled aggregates while preventing harmful chemical cross-contamination in secondary building materials.

The Challenge of Mixed Demolition Waste

When a building is demolished, the resulting rubble is a complex mixture of concrete, masonry, wood, and plaster. To recycle clean brick aggregate for reuse in new construction or road bases, contaminants like gypsum drywall and autoclaved aerated concrete must be completely removed.

Both gypsum and AAC contain high levels of sulfates. If these materials remain mixed with crushed brick and concrete, the sulfates react with moisture and cement compounds to form ettringite. This mineral expands as it forms, causing structural cracking and failure in newly poured concrete. Traditional sorting methods, such as water floatation or manual picking, are often too slow, expensive, or inaccurate to separate these similarly weighted and dusty materials effectively.

Material Detection via Laser-Induced Fluorescence

Laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) solves the identification problem by looking at the molecular composition of the rubble rather than its surface color or density. As crushed demolition waste travels down a high-speed conveyor belt, it passes under a specialized laser scanner.

  • Excitation: The laser emits ultraviolet or visible light pulses onto the moving fragments.
  • Fluorescence: The minerals within the waste absorb this laser energy and instantly re-emit light at different wavelengths. This is known as fluorescence.
  • Spectral Signatures: Each material has a unique chemical makeup that produces a distinct fluorescent glow and decay time. Calcium sulfate in gypsum drywall, the aluminum-induced porous calcium silicate hydrates in AAC, and the fired clay minerals in brick all emit entirely different spectral signatures.

Because the laser penetrates surface dust—a common issue in demolition recycling—LIF sensors can accurately read the material's core identity in milliseconds.

Artificial Intelligence Processing

Once the optical sensors capture the fluorescent light emitted by the rubble, the data is fed directly into an artificial intelligence system. The AI utilizes machine learning algorithms and neural networks trained on vast datasets of construction materials.

The AI processes the spectral data alongside spatial information to determine three things simultaneously:

  1. Material Type: Whether the scanned object is gypsum, AAC, or clean brick.
  2. Geometry and Mass: The exact size, shape, and estimated weight of the individual fragment.
  3. Belt Position: The precise coordinates and speed of the object moving along the conveyor belt.

By fusing LIF data with AI pattern recognition, the system overcomes anomalies like wet surfaces, painted drywall, or mortar attached to bricks, making split-second classification decisions.

Precision Pneumatic Ejection

The final stage of the separation process occurs at the end of the conveyor belt, where the material falls freely through the air. Positioned just below the belt's edge is a bar containing hundreds of high-pressure air nozzles.

The AI system acts as the brain for this pneumatic ejection manifold. Knowing the exact speed and trajectory of a falling piece of waste, the AI calculates the precise millisecond a fragment of gypsum or AAC will pass in front of a specific nozzle.

  • Center-of-Mass Targeting: The AI triggers a targeted blast of compressed air aimed directly at the fragment's center of mass. For larger pieces of brick or AAC, the AI may fire multiple adjacent nozzles simultaneously to ensure enough force is applied.
  • Separation Chutes: The air blast alters the trajectory of the targeted contaminant, blowing the gypsum or AAC over a splitter wall into designated collection chutes. Meanwhile, the clean brick aggregate is allowed to fall undisturbed along its natural trajectory into a separate bin—or vice versa, depending on the facility's setup.

Operating at speeds of several meters per second, this combination of LIF, AI, and pneumatic ejection allows recyclers to process tons of mixed rubble per hour, turning unusable demolition waste into certified, high-grade building materials.
