Why Vanishing and Exploding Gradients Hindered Deep AI

For years, the development of deep artificial intelligence networks was severely bottlenecked by two mathematical phenomena known as vanishing and exploding gradients. These issues occur during the training process when error signals are sent backward through a network's layers to update its knowledge. In deep networks with many layers, these signals either shrink until they disappear completely or grow exponentially until they destabilize the entire system. This article explores the mechanics behind these gradient problems and explains exactly how they prevented early deep neural networks from learning effectively.

To understand why gradients fail, it is first necessary to understand how a neural network learns. Networks learn through a process called backpropagation. When a network makes a prediction, it compares the result to the correct answer to calculate an error. It then works backward from the final output layer to the initial input layer, using calculus—specifically the chain rule—to determine how much each internal connection (weight) contributed to the error. The mathematical value used to adjust these weights is called the gradient.

Because of the chain rule, calculating the gradient for the earliest layers in a network requires multiplying many numbers together, one for each layer the signal passes through. This repeated multiplication is the root cause of both vanishing and exploding gradients.

The vanishing gradient problem happens when the numbers being multiplied together are less than one. Historically, neural networks relied on activation functions like the sigmoid or hyperbolic tangent (tanh) functions. These functions squash large input numbers into a very small range, which means their derivatives—the numbers used in the backward multiplication—are always small fractions.

When you multiply a fraction by another fraction repeatedly across dozens of layers, the resulting number shrinks exponentially. By the time the error signal reaches the early layers of the network, the gradient is virtually zero. Because the gradient dictates how much the network should adjust its weights, a gradient of zero means the early layers stop learning entirely. Since the earliest layers are responsible for learning the most fundamental features of the data, a network with frozen early layers becomes completely useless, rendering the "deep" part of the deep network ineffective.

Conversely, the exploding gradient problem occurs when the numbers multiplied during backpropagation are greater than one. This usually happens if the initial weights of the network are set too high. When numbers greater than one are multiplied together across many layers, the gradient grows exponentially.

By the time the signal reaches the early layers, the gradient is massive. Instead of making small, precise adjustments to the network's weights, the algorithm makes drastic, wild changes. This causes the network to overshoot its learning targets, resulting in an unstable system that cannot converge on a working solution. In extreme cases, the numbers become so large that they exceed the computer's memory limits, resulting in "NaN" (Not a Number) errors that completely crash the training process.

Ultimately, vanishing and exploding gradients forced early AI researchers to stick to shallow networks with only a few layers, delaying the deep learning revolution. It was only when researchers introduced new techniques—such as the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function, smarter weight initialization methods, batch normalization, and residual connections (skip connections)—that AI was finally able to bypass these mathematical roadblocks and successfully train the massive, deep networks we rely on today.
