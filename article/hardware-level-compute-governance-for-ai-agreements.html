Hardware-Level Compute Governance for AI Agreements

Compute governance proposes utilizing physical and cryptographic mechanisms embedded directly into high-end artificial intelligence chips to enforce and verify compliance with international AI safety treaties. Because advanced AI models require massive amounts of computational power, regulating the physical hardware is the most reliable bottle-neck for monitoring AI development. This article explores how proposed hardware-level mechanisms—such as secure enclaves, cryptographic licensing, workload attestation, and physical networking limits—enable regulators to track AI chip usage, restrict unauthorized training runs, and verify treaty compliance without exposing proprietary data.

Cryptographic Licensing and Usage Throttling

The foundational mechanism of hardware-enabled governance is cryptographic licensing. Under this system, advanced AI processors are manufactured with a default state that locks their maximum performance capabilities. To unlock the chip for large-scale AI training, the operator must input a cryptographic key issued by an international regulatory body.

These keys act as time-bound or compute-bound licenses. A chip’s internal counters track the number of floating-point operations (FLOPs) executed. Once the allocated compute budget is exhausted, the hardware automatically throttles its performance or halts operations until a new cryptographic authorization is provided. This ensures that no entity can secretly amass enough compute to train a frontier model beyond agreed-upon international thresholds.

Confidential Computing and Workload Attestation

To verify that a data center is only running approved AI models without violating the operator's commercial privacy, compute governance relies on confidential computing and remote attestation. Modern chips can be equipped with a hardware Root of Trust (RoT) and Secure Enclaves—isolated areas on the silicon that cannot be altered by the user or the operating system.

As an AI training run occurs, the chip generates a cryptographic hash (a unique digital fingerprint) of the workload and the amount of compute used. Through remote attestation, the chip signs this data with an unforgeable private key baked into the silicon during manufacturing and sends the report to international inspectors. Regulators can then verify that the compute usage matches declared activities without ever seeing the proprietary code or sensitive training data.

Interconnect and Clustering Limits

A single AI chip cannot train a frontier model; it requires thousands of chips linked together in a supercomputing cluster. Therefore, hardware governance proposes strict controls over chip-to-chip communication.

Chips can be designed with firmware that requires cryptographic handshakes before they can communicate with neighboring processors via high-speed interconnects. By limiting the number of chips that can legally link together without a regulatory key, hardware mechanisms can physically prevent the formation of unauthorized superclusters. A facility might legally own thousands of chips to run smaller, permitted inference tasks, but the hardware itself will refuse to network at the bandwidth required for a massive, unapproved training run.

Location Verification and Tamper Resistance

To enforce export controls and ensure chips remain within compliant jurisdictions, processors can integrate secure location verification. Rather than relying on easily spoofed GPS signals, chips can use cryptographic pinging—measuring the exact millisecond delay (latency) of signals sent between the chip and secure, geographically fixed regulatory servers—to triangulate their physical location. If a chip is moved to an unauthorized facility, it automatically locks down.

Finally, because bad actors might attempt to physically alter the silicon to bypass these digital locks, governance-ready chips require physical tamper-resistance. Techniques such as protective meshes that trigger a self-brick mechanism (permanently erasing the chip’s cryptographic keys) if the casing is opened or probed with lasers ensure that the hardware-level mechanisms remain intact and enforceable.
