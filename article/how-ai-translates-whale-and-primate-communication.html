How AI Translates Whale and Primate Communication

Artificial intelligence is revolutionizing the field of bioacoustics by enabling scientists to decode complex animal vocalizations like never before. Through advanced machine learning algorithms and natural language processing models, researchers are analyzing massive datasets of audio and behavioral recordings to uncover hidden structures in nonhuman communication. This article explores the mechanics of how AI translates these intricate acoustic signals, focusing specifically on breakthrough studies in marine mammal and primate communication that are bridging the gap between human and animal languages.

The Mechanics of AI in Bioacoustics

Traditional bioacoustics relied heavily on researchers manually reviewing spectrograms—visual representations of sound frequencies over time. This process was exceptionally slow and vulnerable to human bias. Today, AI automates and expands this work through a multi-step analytical pipeline:

  • Data Collection: Scientists deploy arrays of underwater hydrophones, canopy microphones, and non-invasive acoustic tags attached directly to animals. These devices capture thousands of hours of raw audio alongside behavioral data, such as movement and social proximity.
  • Noise Filtering and Segmentation: Machine learning models automatically scrub background noise—like ocean currents, boat engines, or rainforest wind—and isolate individual animal vocalizations from the continuous audio stream.
  • Feature Extraction: Deep learning networks convert these sounds into mathematical vectors, mapping subtle acoustic features such as pitch, duration, rhythm, timbre, and frequency modulation that the human ear cannot detect.
  • Pattern Recognition: Using the same transformer architectures that power human large language models (LLMs), the AI clusters similar calls together and analyzes the sequential order of the sounds. It looks for syntax, grammar-like rules, and how specific sounds correlate with documented physical behaviors.

Decoding Whale Vocalizations

Whales possess some of the most sophisticated acoustic systems in the animal kingdom, making them prime candidates for AI translation. The most prominent initiative in this space is Project CETI (Cetacean Translation Initiative), which uses advanced machine learning to study the click-based communication of sperm whales.

Sperm whales communicate using "codas," which are distinct patterns of rapid clicks. For decades, scientists believed whales only had a limited repertoire of these codas. By applying AI to analyze thousands of recordings, researchers recently discovered a "sperm whale phonetic alphabet." The AI identified that whales systematically alter the rhythm, tempo, and duration of their clicks—and even add extra ornamentation clicks—to create hundreds of distinct combinations.

By pairing these acoustic findings with tracking data from underwater drones and tags, the AI maps which codas are used during specific activities, such as deep-sea foraging, socializing at the surface, or nursing calves. Similarly, researchers studying humpback whales use neural networks to track how complex whale songs evolve and spread culturally across different ocean basins over time.

Translating Primate Communication

Unlike marine mammals, primates rely heavily on multimodal communication, combining vocalizations with facial expressions, posture, and hand gestures. To translate primate communication, researchers use a combination of acoustic AI and computer vision.

When studying species like chimpanzees, bonobos, and vervet monkeys, AI algorithms analyze both audio recordings and video footage simultaneously. The acoustic models categorize grunts, pant-hoots, barks, and screams, while computer vision tracks the physical context of the interaction.

Through this combined approach, AI has helped scientists confirm that primate communication is far more intentional and structured than mere emotional reflexes. Key breakthroughs include:

  • Referential Signaling: Machine learning models have verified that certain primates use specific acoustic variations to warn others about distinct types of predators, such as eagles versus leopards, prompting different evasive actions from the group.
  • Syntax and Combination: AI analysis shows that primates combine specific calls into sequences that alter the original meaning of the individual sounds, a foundational element of human syntax.
  • Dialect Detection: Algorithms have successfully identified regional dialects among separated groups of the same primate species, proving that parts of their vocal communication are socially learned rather than purely genetic.

Moving From Pattern Recognition to True Translation

Finding patterns in animal sounds is only the first step; true translation requires understanding semantic meaning. To achieve this, AI models must ground acoustic patterns in behavioral reality. If an AI predicts that a specific sequence of whale clicks or primate grunts means "food is nearby" or "move this way," it validates this by checking if the animals consistently perform that action after the sound is made.

In the future, researchers aim to use generative AI to conduct interactive playback experiments. By synthesizing realistic whale codas or primate calls and broadcasting them back to the animals, scientists can observe their reactions to verify the accuracy of the AI's translations, moving humanity closer to two-way interspecies communication.
