AI Video Architecture: Temporal Consistency and Physics

Generating realistic video from text prompts requires artificial intelligence to solve two difficult problems: maintaining visual continuity over time and accurately simulating the physical world. Early AI video models simply stitched independent images together, which resulted in severe flickering and morphing objects. Modern text-to-video architectures overcome these hurdles by utilizing specialized frameworks—primarily Diffusion Transformers, 3D compression networks, and spatiotemporal attention mechanisms. This article explores the core architectural techniques that enable AI models to generate smooth, temporally consistent, and physically plausible videos.

3D Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)

Before an AI model can generate video, it must compress massive amounts of visual data into a manageable format known as "latent space." Standard image generators use 2D Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) to compress single images spatially. However, compressing video frame-by-frame ruins temporal consistency before generation even begins.

Text-to-video models instead use 3D VAEs, which compress data across both space (height and width) and time (frames). By grouping multiple frames into a single representation, the model inherently links the timeline together. This prevents the micro-flickering seen in older models because adjacent frames share the same foundational data points.

Spacetime Latent Patches

Modern video architectures, such as OpenAI's Sora, replace traditional pixel grids with "spacetime patches." This technique borrows heavily from how Large Language Models (LLMs) process text using tokens.

A video is first compressed by the 3D VAE and then sliced into three-dimensional cubes—or patches—that contain both spatial visual data and a slice of time. By converting a video into a sequence of spacetime patches, the AI treats video generation similarly to predicting the next word in a sentence. Because each patch spans multiple frames, the continuity of objects and backgrounds is baked directly into the building blocks of the generation process.

Spatiotemporal Attention Mechanisms

To maintain object permanence—ensuring a car looks like the same car from frame one to frame one hundred—models rely on spatiotemporal attention. In standard image generation, "spatial attention" allows the AI to understand how different parts of a single image relate to one another. Video models add "temporal attention" to this equation.

Temporal attention layers force the neural network to look backward and forward across the timeline while generating a specific frame. There are two main ways architectures achieve this:

  • Divided Attention: The model applies spatial attention to a single frame first, and then immediately applies temporal attention across the timeline for that specific region.
  • Full 3D Attention: The model calculates the relationships between all spacetime patches across the entire video simultaneously. While computationally heavy, this yields the highest level of consistency and allows the model to keep track of objects even if they temporarily leave the frame.

Diffusion Transformers (DiTs)

Historically, diffusion models relied on an architecture called a U-Net to gradually turn random noise into a clear image. While effective for static pictures, U-Nets struggle to retain long-term memory across hundreds of video frames.

State-of-the-art video models have largely replaced U-Nets with Diffusion Transformers (DiTs). Transformers are exceptionally good at handling long sequences of data. When paired with spacetime patches, a DiT can process an entire video clip at once rather than generating it strictly frame-by-frame. This global view of the timeline ensures that the beginning, middle, and end of the video remain logically and visually connected.

Emergent Physics Through Scale

Unlike traditional computer graphics engines (such as Unreal Engine), text-to-video AI models generally do not have hardcoded physics engines dictating gravity, fluid dynamics, or momentum. Instead, realistic physics is largely an emergent property of massive scale and DiT architectures.

When a Diffusion Transformer is trained on millions of hours of real-world video, the spatiotemporal attention layers must find the most efficient way to predict how spacetime patches change over time. The AI mathematically deduces that to predict the next patch accurately, it must internalize the rules of the physical world. Consequently, the model learns how light reflects off water, how fabric drapes, and how gravity affects falling objects simply by observing patterns across massive datasets.

Optical Flow and Motion Modules

To further refine physical movement, many architectures integrate explicit motion modules or optical flow conditioning. Optical flow is a computer vision technique that tracks the direction and speed of moving pixels from one frame to the next.

Some architectures separated the generation process into two distinct networks: one that generates the static content, and a specialized "motion module" trained exclusively on trajectories and camera movements. Other models use optical flow maps as an invisible guide during the diffusion process, forcing the generated pixels to follow mathematically smooth trajectories. This prevents objects from teleporting, morphing unnaturally, or violating basic laws of momentum.
