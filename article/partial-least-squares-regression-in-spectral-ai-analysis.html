Partial Least Squares Regression in Spectral AI Analysis

This article provides a comprehensive overview of how Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression resolves extreme multicollinearity in chemometric and spectral artificial intelligence analysis. We will explore the nature of collinearity in high-dimensional spectral datasets, the mathematical mechanics of how PLS extracts orthogonal latent variables, and why this method outperforms traditional regression and standard dimensionality reduction techniques in predictive AI modeling.

The Challenge of Multicollinearity in Spectral Data

In chemometrics and spectral analysis—such as Near-Infrared (NIR), Raman, or Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy—instruments record absorbance or reflectance across thousands of wavelengths (variables) for a relatively small number of physical samples. This creates a "wide" dataset where predictors vastly outnumber observations.

Furthermore, adjacent wavelengths in a spectrum are not independent; if a chemical bond absorbs light at 1500 nm, it will also strongly absorb light at 1501 nm. This phenomenon is known as extreme multicollinearity. When standard algorithms like Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression are applied to this data, the mathematical matrix inversion required to calculate coefficients becomes unstable or impossible. The resulting AI models overfit wildly, rendering them useless for predicting properties in new, unseen samples.

How PLS Regression Solves the Problem

Partial Least Squares regression bypasses the issue of multicollinearity by refusing to use the original, correlated wavelengths directly in the regression model. Instead, it projects the high-dimensional data into a lower-dimensional space using the following step-by-step mechanics:

1. Extraction of Latent Variables

PLS compresses the thousands of correlated spectral variables (\(X\)) into a handful of new, synthetic variables called Latent Variables (LVs) or components. Each LV is a linear combination of the original wavelengths. Instead of feeding 2,000 correlated wavelengths into an AI model, you might only feed 5 to 10 LVs.

2. Maximizing Covariance (The Crucial Step)

Other dimensionality reduction techniques, like Principal Component Analysis (PCA), create components solely by finding the directions of maximum variance within the spectral data (\(X\)). However, the largest variations in a spectrum might just be baseline drift, light scattering, or noise—not the chemical property you are trying to predict (\(Y\)).

PLS solves this by looking at both the spectral data (\(X\)) and the target chemical values (\(Y\)) simultaneously. It constructs Latent Variables that maximize the covariance between \(X\) and \(Y\). This ensures that the compressed variables actually contain the specific spectral signals relevant to the target prediction.

3. Enforcing Orthogonality

This is the exact mechanism that eliminates multicollinearity. As PLS extracts each sequential Latent Variable, it mathematically forces them to be orthogonal (perpendicular) to one another. Because the new Latent Variables are orthogonal, the correlation between them is strictly zero. PLS then performs a standard regression using these uncorrelated Latent Variables instead of the original wavelengths, completely stabilizing the math.

Advantages in Spectral AI Pipelines

Integrating PLS into modern chemometric AI workflows offers three distinct advantages:

  • Noise Reduction: By limiting the model to a small number of Latent Variables (determined via cross-validation), PLS naturally leaves random spectral noise behind in the discarded dimensions, preventing the AI from overfitting.
  • Interpretability via Loadings: Unlike "black-box" deep learning models, PLS provides "loading weights." Scientists can plot these weights to see exactly which original wavelengths contributed most to the Latent Variables, linking the AI's predictions directly back to known chemical bonds.
  • Computational Efficiency: Transforming thousands of correlated features into a few orthogonal components drastically reduces the computational load, allowing for rapid model training and real-time deployment on edge devices and portable spectrometers.
