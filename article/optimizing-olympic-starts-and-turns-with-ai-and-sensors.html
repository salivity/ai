Optimizing Olympic Starts and Turns with AI and Sensors

Olympic swimming and track coaches are turning to advanced technology to gain a competitive edge where medals are decided by fractions of a second. This article explores how wearable inertial measurement units (IMUs) and artificial intelligence (AI) models analyze biomechanical movements and fluid resistance. By tracking micro-movements and hydrodynamic drag profiles, coaches can precisely adjust an athlete's posture, force application, and timing to shave crucial hundredths of a second off race starts and swimming flip turns.

The Role of Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs)

Inertial sensors are small wearable devices equipped with accelerometers, gyroscopes, and magnetometers. Placed on a swimmer’s lower back, wrists, or swim cap, and on a sprinter’s torso or shoes, these sensors capture movement data hundreds of times per second.

Unlike traditional video cameras, which can be obscured by water splashes or limited by frame rates, IMUs provide continuous, real-time data on velocity, acceleration, and body orientation.

  • For Swimmers: Underwater IMUs track the exact angle of water entry, the deceleration rate during the glide phase, and the rotational speed of a flip turn.
  • For Track Sprinters: Sensors measure reaction time out of the blocks, the angle of shin projection, and the exact G-forces generated during the first three steps of the drive phase.

Hydrodynamic Drag-Profile Artificial Intelligence

In swimming, water is nearly 800 times denser than air, making drag the biggest obstacle to speed. Hydrodynamic drag-profile AI uses machine learning algorithms combined with computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to map how water flows around a specific swimmer's body.

Coaches feed high-speed underwater video and IMU data into the AI system. The software then creates a 3D digital twin of the athlete. This model visualizes invisible pressure zones and turbulence caused by the swimmer's body position. If a swimmer’s head is lifted by just two degrees during an underwater streamline, the AI calculates the exact increase in drag and translates that into time lost over 15 meters.

Perfecting the Start

The start of a race requires maximum explosive power paired with minimal resistance. Coaches use sensor and AI data to refine this phase down to the millisecond.

In track, sensors inside the starting blocks and on the athlete measure the horizontal and vertical force vectors. Coaches use this data to find the optimal block spacing and hip height. The goal is to eliminate wasted vertical bounce and direct all force forward, shaving hundredths of a second off the initial 10-meter split.

In swimming, the start combines a track-style block push with a precise water entry. The AI analyzes the dive trajectory to ensure the swimmer enters the water through a single "hole," minimizing splash and surface tension drag. Once underwater, IMU data tells the coach the exact split-second the swimmer’s dive velocity drops to match their swimming speed. This allows the coach to instruct the athlete on the precise moment to initiate their dolphin kick, preventing premature kicking which actually slows the swimmer down.

Shaving Time Off Flip Turns

The flip turn is a blind, complex movement where races are frequently won or lost. Coaches break the turn down into four phases using IMU metrics: the approach, the rotation, the wall contact, and the push-off.

  1. Distance to Wall: Sensors track the deceleration prior to the wall, helping coaches establish the exact distance at which the swimmer should initiate the tuck.
  2. Rotational Velocity: Gyroscopes measure how fast the swimmer flips. A tighter tuck reduces the moment of inertia, resulting in a faster spin.
  3. Wall Contact Time: Just like a track start, spending too long on the wall costs time, while pushing off too early sacrifices power. Sensors help find the sweet spot—usually around 0.3 seconds of foot contact—for maximum force generation.
  4. The Streamline Glide: Hydrodynamic AI evaluates the push-off angle. If the swimmer pushes off too deep or too shallow, they encounter wave drag or waste energy swimming upward. The AI dictates the exact depth and streamline posture required to maintain peak velocity back to the surface.

Real-Time Feedback at the Pool and Track

The ultimate advantage of these technologies is immediate feedback. Data from the IMUs is transmitted via Bluetooth to a coach's tablet the moment a swimmer surfaces or a sprinter finishes a rep. Instead of relying on intuition, a coach can tell an athlete to tuck their chin by one inch or shift their weight forward by five percent, immediately validating the time saved on the very next attempt.
