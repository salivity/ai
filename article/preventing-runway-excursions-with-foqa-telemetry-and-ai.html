Preventing Runway Excursions with FOQA Telemetry and AI

Aviation safety analysts prevent runway excursions by combining Flight Operational Quality Assurance (FOQA) programs, Quick-Access Recorder (QAR) telemetry, and anomaly-detection artificial intelligence. By continuously harvesting high-resolution flight data from every routine flight, airlines create a massive repository of operational metrics. Advanced machine learning algorithms then analyze this telemetry to identify subtle, multivariate patterns that precede unstable approaches. This proactive methodology allows safety teams to spot hidden operational risks, adjust pilot training, and modify approach procedures before an actual runway incident occurs.

Capturing Telemetry with Quick-Access Recorders

The foundation of modern predictive aviation safety is the Quick-Access Recorder. Unlike traditional black boxes designed strictly for crash survival and post-accident investigation, QARs are built for easy, routine data extraction by maintenance and safety crews.

During a flight, the QAR records thousands of parameters per second. This telemetry includes airspeed, altitude, vertical speed, pitch and roll angles, thrust lever positions, flap configurations, and precise flight control inputs. Once an aircraft lands at the gate, this data is offloaded—often wirelessly—into the airline’s centralized FOQA database for analysis.

The Limits of Traditional FOQA

Historically, FOQA relied on exceedance detection. Safety analysts programmed software with strict, predefined thresholds based on the airline's standard operating procedures. For example, if an aircraft descended below 1,000 feet above ground level with a sink rate exceeding 1,000 feet per minute, the software flagged it as an unstable approach.

While useful, this method only identifies single-variable anomalies that cross a hard line. It completely misses flights where multiple parameters are simultaneous borderline issues. A flight might have a slightly high airspeed, a marginally late flap extension, and minor tailwind fluctuations. Individually, none of these trigger a traditional FOQA alert, yet together they severely degrade the safety margin and set the stage for a runway excursion.

How Anomaly-Detection AI Identifies Unstable Patterns

To catch complex precursor events, safety analysts deploy unsupervised machine learning and anomaly-detection AI. Instead of looking for pre-programmed rule violations, the AI learns what a normal approach looks like by analyzing millions of historical landings at a specific runway.

The AI maps the multivariate relationships between hundreds of QAR parameters simultaneously during the approach phase. It establishes a baseline of normal energy management. When a flight deviates from this complex baseline, the AI flags it as an statistical anomaly.

For instance, the AI can detect a scenario where a pilot is making rapid, oscillating pitch and thrust inputs to maintain the glideslope. Even if the aircraft's airspeed and descent rate remain strictly within standard limits, the AI recognizes that the pilot's workload is abnormally high and the aircraft's kinetic energy is poorly managed. These subtle patterns are the exact precursors to unstabilized approaches that result in long landings or high-speed runway overruns.

Turning AI Insights into Prevention

When the AI isolates a cluster of anomalous approaches, aviation safety analysts investigate the underlying causes to prevent a future runway excursion. The corrective actions generally fall into three categories:

  • Airspace and Procedure Redesign: If the AI reveals a high concentration of energy-management anomalies on a specific runway, analysts often find external causes. Air Traffic Control at that specific airport might be keeping aircraft too high or too fast before turning onto the final approach. Airlines use this telemetry data to work with aviation authorities to redesign arrival procedures.
  • Targeted Simulator Training: If the telemetry shows that fleets are struggling with specific weather phenomena, such as shifting tailwinds during approach, safety teams update flight simulator scenarios. Pilots are then trained specifically on recognizing and correcting those exact energy-state anomalies, and reinforcing the decision to execute a go-around.
  • Fleet-Wide Briefings: De-identified data trends are shared directly with flight crews. Pilots receive briefings on which airports and approaches currently present the highest statistical risk for an unstable approach, heightening their situational awareness before they even push back from the gate.

By shifting from reactive threshold monitoring to predictive AI pattern recognition, aviation analysts effectively neutralize the hidden precursors of unstable approaches long before an aircraft runs out of runway.
