Why AI Data Centers Are Switching to Liquid Cooling

The rapid rise of artificial intelligence has created an unprecedented surge in computing power, pushing traditional data center infrastructure to its physical limits. As AI processors generate vastly more heat than standard servers, legacy air-cooling systems can no longer keep up. To prevent hardware failure, maximize performance, and reduce energy consumption, AI data centers are rapidly transitioning from air cooling to advanced liquid cooling methods, specifically direct-to-chip cold plates and two-phase immersion cooling.

The Limits of Traditional Air Cooling

For decades, data centers relied on fans and chillers to circulate cold air through server racks. This method was sufficient when server racks consumed between 5 and 15 kilowatts (kW) of power. However, modern AI workloads—particularly large language model training—require clusters of graphics processing units (GPUs) that consume 40 kW to over 100 kW per rack.

Air is a poor conductor of heat. At these extreme power densities, fans simply cannot move enough air fast enough to absorb the heat being generated by the silicon. Furthermore, the energy required to power massive air-conditioning units and high-speed fans drives up operational costs and ruins the data center's Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE). Liquid, by contrast, can conduct heat up to 3,000 times more efficiently than air, making it the only viable solution for next-generation AI hardware.

Direct-to-Chip Cold Plates: The Immediate Solution

Direct-to-chip (D2C) cooling, also known as cold plate cooling, is currently the most popular transition technology for AI data centers. Instead of cooling the entire room, this method brings liquid directly to the hottest components inside the server, primarily the GPUs and CPUs.

A metal cold plate sits directly on top of the processor. A dielectric fluid or treated water is pumped through microchannels inside the plate, absorbing the heat directly from the chip. The heated liquid is then pumped away to a heat exchanger to be cooled and recirculated.

AI data centers favor direct-to-chip cooling because it does not require a complete redesign of the facility. Operators can still use standard server racks and familiar maintenance procedures, making it an easy, cost-effective bridge between legacy air systems and full liquid adoption. However, because D2C only cools the main processors, fans are still needed to cool secondary components like power supplies and memory.

Two-Phase Immersion Cooling: The Ultimate Efficiency

For the highest-density AI workloads, data centers are adopting two-phase immersion cooling. This method eliminates fans, air handlers, and cold plates entirely. Instead, whole servers are submerged in a specially designed, sealed bath filled with a thermally conductive, non-conductive (dielectric) liquid. Because the fluid does not conduct electricity, it will not damage the electronic components.

The "two-phase" process relies on the physics of boiling and condensation:

  1. Phase One (Liquid to Gas): The submerged AI chips generate intense heat, causing the surrounding fluid—which has a very low boiling point—to boil. As the liquid turns into vapor, it absorbs a massive amount of thermal energy away from the servers.
  2. Phase Two (Gas to Liquid): The heated vapor rises to the top of the sealed tank, where it hits a water-cooled condenser coil. The vapor cools, condenses back into a liquid, and drips back down into the bath to repeat the cycle.

Two-phase immersion cooling is adopted because it provides total thermal uniformity. Every component on the server board is cooled evenly, eliminating hotspots and extending the lifespan of the expensive AI hardware. Additionally, because the phase-change process is passive and relies on natural convection rather than heavy-duty pumps and fans, it drastically reduces the data center's overall electricity consumption.

Space and Sustainability Benefits

Beyond managing chip temperatures, the transition to liquid cooling solves two major business problems for AI companies: real estate and sustainability.

Air-cooled servers require significant physical space between racks to allow for airflow. Liquid cooling allows operators to pack servers much closer together, drastically increasing the computing power per square foot. Furthermore, by eliminating energy-hungry chillers and fans, liquid cooling significantly lowers the carbon footprint of AI operations, allowing tech companies to scale their artificial intelligence capabilities while still meeting strict environmental regulations.
