Why Greedy Decoding and Beam Search Cause AI Text Loops

In open-ended artificial intelligence text generation, relying on deterministic algorithms like greedy decoding and pure beam search frequently leads to repetitive degeneration loops. While these methods are designed to select the most probable words and sentences, this exact prioritization of high probability is what causes the AI to get stuck repeating the same phrases indefinitely. This article explores the mechanics behind why maximizing likelihood breaks down in creative generation, the self-reinforcing nature of transformer attention, and how contrasting these methods with natural human language patterns explains the phenomenon of text degeneration.

The Mechanics of Greedy Decoding and Beam Search

To understand why loops occur, it is first necessary to understand how these decoding methods choose words. Language models predict the probability of the next token (word or word piece) based on all previous tokens.

Greedy decoding is the simplest approach: at every single step, the algorithm selects the one token with the absolute highest probability. Pure beam search is slightly more advanced, keeping track of a fixed number of the most probable sequences (beams) at each step and ultimately choosing the sequence with the highest overall combined probability. Both methods share a single goal: maximize the mathematical likelihood of the generated text.

The Likelihood Trap in Open-Ended Generation

Maximizing probability works exceptionally well for directed tasks with low uncertainty, such as machine translation or summarization, where there is a narrow range of correct answers. However, in open-ended text generation, there is no single correct path.

Research into natural language processing reveals a stark difference between how humans speak and how probability-maximizing algorithms behave. Human language is inherently unpredictable. People naturally weave between high-probability words (like prepositions and articles) and low-probability words (like unique adjectives and novel nouns) to convey new information.

When an algorithm strictly forces the selection of the highest-probability tokens, it actively avoids the low-probability words that introduce variety and drive a narrative forward. The generated text quickly becomes generic, flat, and stripped of the natural variance required to transition to new topics.

Self-Reinforcing Attention Loops

The transition from generic text to an infinite loop is largely driven by the architecture of modern Transformer models. Transformers use a mechanism called "self-attention" to look back at previous tokens in the context window to decide what comes next.

If greedy decoding or beam search selects a slightly repetitive phrase because it is statistically safe, that phrase now exists in the recent context. When the model calculates probabilities for the next tokens, the self-attention mechanism notices the pattern. Because language models are trained to recognize and continue patterns, the probability of repeating that exact same phrase again increases.

Once the phrase is generated a second time, the pattern becomes even stronger in the context window. The probability of repeating it a third time approaches certainty. This creates a positive feedback loop—a mathematical sinkhole where the most probable next sequence is always the exact sequence that was just generated.

The Positive Feedback of High-Probability Tokens

Furthermore, certain common words and phrases naturally carry high transition probabilities to one another. For example, phrases like "I don't know" or "It is important to note that" are heavily represented in training data.

When a deterministic search algorithm wanders into one of these common phrasing structures, the safest statistical path is often circular. Beam search, in particular, exacerbates this because it optimizes for sequence-level probability. A repetitive loop of high-probability words will mathematically score much higher than a creative, varied sentence that temporarily dips into lower-probability tokens. Consequently, beam search will actively prune away the diverse, natural-sounding beams in favor of the repetitive, high-scoring loop.

Overcoming Degeneration with Stochastic Sampling

To prevent these repetitive loops, modern AI systems abandon pure likelihood maximization in open-ended tasks. Instead, they rely on stochastic (random) sampling methods, such as Top-k and Top-p (nucleus) sampling, combined with temperature adjustments. By introducing controlled randomness and truncating the long tail of completely irrelevant words, these methods allow the AI to occasionally select lower-probability tokens, successfully mimicking the surprising cadence of human language and breaking the mathematical cycle of repetition.
