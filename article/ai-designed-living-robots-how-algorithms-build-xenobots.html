AI-Designed Living Robots: How Algorithms Build Xenobots

This article explores the intersection of evolutionary artificial intelligence and synthetic biology, detailing how computer algorithms design living robots known as xenobots and anthrobots. By simulating biological physics, AI creates blueprints for multicellular structures assembled from frog or human cells. Readers will learn how evolutionary algorithms optimize these biological designs in virtual environments, how scientists physically assemble the living tissues in the lab, and the differences between amphibian-based xenobots and human-derived anthrobots.

The Role of Evolutionary Algorithms

Designing a biological machine from scratch is difficult because living cells have complex behaviors that are hard to predict. To solve this, scientists use evolutionary algorithms. These are AI programs inspired by natural selection.

The process begins in a physics-based virtual environment. Scientists give the AI a specific goal, such as moving a structure from one point to another as quickly as possible. The algorithm generates thousands of random 3D configurations using two types of virtual building blocks: passive blocks that hold their shape, and active blocks that expand and contract.

Virtual Testing and Selection

Once the AI generates the initial random designs, it tests them in the simulator. Most of the early designs fail to move. However, a few configurations manage to inch forward due to the specific placement of their contracting blocks.

The evolutionary algorithm keeps the top-performing designs and deletes the failures. It then creates a new generation of designs by slightly mutating and combining the successful ones. After running this cycle hundreds or thousands of times, the AI produces optimized, functional blueprints for living machines that can walk, swim, or push objects.

Assembling Xenobots from Frog Cells

With the AI-generated blueprints complete, biologists move the process from the computer to the laboratory to create xenobots. The building blocks are harvested from the embryos of the African clawed frog (Xenopus laevis).

Scientists use two specific types of cells to match the virtual blocks used by the AI:

  • Skin cells (epidermal): These act as the passive, structural framework.
  • Heart muscle cells (cardiomyocytes): These act as the active engines, as they naturally contract and relax on their own.

Using microscopic forceps and electrodes, researchers painstakingly shape the harvested cells to match the exact 3D blueprint created by the AI. Once placed together, the cells naturally bond. Driven by the rhythmic contractions of the heart cells and guided by the shape of the skin cells, the xenobots move across a petri dish just as the AI predicted.

Transitioning to Human Anthrobots

Following the success of xenobots, researchers advanced the technology to create anthrobots using adult human cells. Unlike xenobots, which require embryonic frog cells and manual shaping with micro-tools, anthrobots are made from adult human tracheal (windpipe) cells and can self-assemble.

While the foundational understanding of cellular mechanics relies on the previous AI simulations, anthrobots leverage the natural abilities of human airway cells. These cells are covered in hair-like projections called cilia, which normally push mucus out of the lungs.

By manipulating the chemical environment in the lab, scientists prompt these cells to multiply and form multicellular spheres with the cilia facing outward. The cilia act as microscopic oars, allowing the anthrobots to swim.

Practical Applications of Living Robots

The combination of AI design and biological assembly opens new frontiers in medicine and environmental science. Because xenobots and anthrobots are made of living tissue, they are fully biodegradable and crumble into harmless dead cells after a few weeks.

In medicine, anthrobots built from a patient's own cells could be injected into the body without triggering an immune response. Under AI-optimized designs, they could be used to clear plaque from arteries, deliver drugs to specific tissues, or help heal damaged nerves—a capability anthrobots have already demonstrated in lab dish experiments. In the environment, xenobots could be deployed to collect microplastics in water or detect toxic chemicals.
