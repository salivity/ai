F1 Pit Strategy: Monte Carlo Sims and Safety Car AI

In modern Formula 1, winning a Grand Prix often happens on the pit wall rather than on the asphalt. Race strategists utilize advanced mathematical frameworks and machine learning to make split-second decisions. By combining Monte Carlo tire-degradation simulations with artificial intelligence that predicts safety-car probabilities, teams can calculate the exact moment to execute an undercut or overcut pit stop, securing track position within mere tenths of a second.

Monte Carlo Simulations for Tire Degradation

Tire degradation is one of the most unpredictable variables in motorsport. As rubber wears down and thermally degrades, lap times get slower. Because exact wear rates depend on fluctuating track temperatures, fuel loads, and driver inputs, strategists cannot rely on a single static prediction. Instead, they use Monte Carlo simulations.

Before and during the race, supercomputers run millions of virtual race scenarios. The Monte Carlo method assigns probability distributions to uncertain variables, particularly tire life and pace drop-off. For every lap, the system simulates thousands of potential outcomes based on real-time telemetry coming from the car's sensors.

This process generates a probability curve showing the exact lap where the time lost on old tires exceeds the time lost by entering the pit lane (typically 20 to 25 seconds). As the race progresses, live data is fed back into the model, narrowing the simulation's variance to tenths of a second.

AI and Safety Car Probability

A perfectly timed pit stop can be ruined—or gifted—by a Safety Car (SC) or Virtual Safety Car (VSC). Because cars must drive at reduced speeds during a neutralization, pitting under a Safety Car costs roughly 40% to 50% less race time relative to the competition than pitting under green-flag conditions.

To anticipate these moments, teams use artificial intelligence trained on decades of historical race data. The AI analyzes multiple real-time inputs to output a live percentage chance of a race neutralization:

  • Track characteristics: Street circuits like Monaco or Baku have higher baseline probabilities due to close walls.
  • Telemetry anomalies: Sudden drops in a rival car’s oil pressure or erratic throttle traces suggest an impending mechanical retirement.
  • Driver proximity: Tight battles in the midfield increase the statistical likelihood of a collision.
  • Weather radar: Incoming rain spikes the probability of aquaplaning incidents.

When the AI detects a spike in Safety Car probability, the strategy software immediately adjusts the value of staying out versus pitting, preparing the crew for an instant reaction.

Executing the Undercut and Overcut

The ultimate goal of combining Monte Carlo data and Safety Car AI is to execute the undercut or the overcut. Both maneuvers are designed to pass a rival car using pit stop timing rather than an on-track overtake.

The Undercut

An undercut occurs when a trailing car pits earlier than the car ahead to bolt on fresh, faster tires. The strategist's software monitors the rival's pace and calculates the "pit window."

To make the call within tenths of a second, the Monte Carlo simulation must confirm three things:

  1. The fresh tires will warm up instantly and provide a massive one-lap pace advantage (the "out-lap").
  2. The driver will exit the pit lane into clean air, completely free of slower traffic.
  3. The time gained on the single out-lap will be greater than the gap to the rival ahead, allowing the trailing car to pass the rival while the rival is pitting on the subsequent lap.

The Overcut

An overcut is the exact opposite: staying out on older tires while the rival pits first. Strategists trigger an overcut when the simulations show that new tires are struggling to reach optimal operating temperatures, or when the rival is projected to exit the pits into heavy traffic.

Furthermore, if the AI indicates a high probability of a Safety Car within the next three laps, the strategist will delay the pit stop. By staying out, the driver sets fast laps in clean air on a light fuel load, hoping to catch a cheap pit stop under a neutralization, jumping the rival who already pitted at full cost.

Making the Sub-Second Call

During the race, all this data is distilled into a unified visual dashboard on the pit wall. Strategists do not read raw code; they look at shifting crossing lines on a graph that represent the predicted finishing positions and time gaps of all 20 cars.

When a rival approaches the pit entry, the system takes less than a second to cross-reference the Monte Carlo tire crossover point with the AI's traffic and safety car models. If the math favors a reaction, an automated alert triggers, and the race engineer instructs the driver to "Box, box" or "Stay out" just meters before the pit lane commitment line.
