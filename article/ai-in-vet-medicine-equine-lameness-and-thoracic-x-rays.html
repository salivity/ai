AI in Vet Medicine: Equine Lameness and Thoracic X-Rays

This article provides an overview of how veterinary medicine utilizes advanced artificial intelligence to transform animal diagnostics. Specifically, it explores how equine veterinarians use motion-capture kinematic AI to detect subtle lameness in horses, and how veterinary radiologists use automated thoracic X-ray screening to identify heart disease in companion animals. By integrating these technologies, veterinary professionals can eliminate diagnostic subjectivity, identify medical conditions much earlier, and begin accurate treatments faster than ever before.

Equine veterinarians face a major challenge when diagnosing subtle lameness. The human eye operates at a limited frame rate, making it difficult to detect minor asymmetries in a horse's movement. Traditional visual gait assessments rely heavily on the observer's experience, which often leads to differing opinions among veterinarians. Motion-capture kinematic AI removes this guesswork by providing objective, biomechanical data.

To use kinematic AI, veterinarians apply wireless inertial sensors to specific anatomical landmarks on the horse, or they use high-speed smartphone cameras paired with computer vision software. As the horse trots, the AI tracks the exact trajectory of the head, withers, and pelvis at hundreds of frames per second.

The algorithm measures vertical displacement to the millimeter. It compares the downward movement of the horse's body during the impact phase of a stride against the upward movement during the push-off phase. If a horse has mild pain in a specific limb, it will shift its weight almost imperceptibly to avoid pressure. The AI detects this minute shift, calculates the exact percentage of asymmetry, and tells the veterinarian which limb is affected and whether the pain occurs when the hoof hits the ground or pushes off. This allows for precise nerve blocking and targeted imaging.

In small animal practice, veterinary radiologists and general practitioners use a different type of artificial intelligence—computer vision and deep learning—to screen thoracic X-rays for heart disease in dogs and cats. Interpreting chest radiographs is notoriously complex because soft tissue structures overlap, and heart shapes vary wildly across different breeds.

Automated thoracic X-ray screening solves this by instantly analyzing digital radiographs the moment they are captured. The AI is trained on ratusan of thousands of previously diagnosed veterinary X-rays. When a new X-ray is uploaded, the algorithm identifies the borders of the heart and the surrounding thoracic cavity.

A primary function of this AI is calculating the Vertebral Heart Score (VHS). Historically, a veterinarian had to manually measure the long and short axes of the heart and compare them against the length of the animal's thoracic vertebrae to check for cardiomegaly (an enlarged heart). The AI automates this geometric calculation in seconds, eliminating human measurement error.

Beyond measuring heart size, the AI evaluates the surrounding lung fields. It scans for abnormal radiopacity, which indicates pulmonary edema—fluid in the lungs caused by congestive heart failure. The software also checks for the elevation of the trachea and the compression of the mainstem bronchi, both of which are classic signs of left atrial enlargement in dogs with mitral valve disease.

For veterinary radiologists, this automated screening acts as a triage system and a second pair of eyes. The AI flags abnormal radiographs for immediate review, ensuring that animals in critical condition receive priority reporting. For general practitioners without an on-site radiologist, the AI provides immediate, data-backed confidence to start life-saving cardiac medications right away.
