Smart Elevators: AI and Clustering in Supertall Skyscrapers

Moving thousands of people rapidly through supertall skyscrapers during the morning rush hour requires more than just fast cables and motors. Modern vertical transportation relies on advanced elevator dispatch systems that utilize destination-hall-call clustering and traffic-pattern artificial intelligence (AI). By grouping passengers heading to the same floors before they even step into an elevator cab and predicting building-wide rush-hour surges based on historical data, these intelligent systems dramatically reduce elevator stops, eliminate lobby bottlenecks, and minimize passenger wait times.

Traditional elevators use a simple two-button system (up or down) in the hallway. The elevator does not know where passengers are going until they step inside and press a floor button. During a morning rush hour in a supertall building, this creates chaos. A single elevator cab might fill up with ten passengers going to ten different floors, resulting in a slow journey with frequent stops and a long delay before the cab can return to the ground lobby.

To solve this, modern skyscrapers use Destination Dispatch Systems (DDS) powered by destination-hall-call clustering. Instead of pressing an "up" button, passengers enter their exact destination floor on a kiosk or turnstile in the main lobby. The system instantly processes these requests using clustering algorithms. It groups passengers traveling to the same floor, or tightly grouped adjacent floors, and directs them to the exact same elevator cab. Because each cab only services a very small cluster of floors on a given trip, the elevator makes far fewer stops, drastically reducing total transit time and allowing the cab to return to the lobby much faster to pick up the next wave of commuters.

While clustering handles the real-time grouping of passengers, traffic-pattern artificial intelligence handles the anticipation of demand. AI algorithms continuously record and analyze passenger flow data, including turnstile swipes, time-of-day usage, and even specific tenant schedules. Over time, the AI learns the unique rhythm of the skyscraper. For example, it might learn that employees for a financial firm on the 60th floor consistently arrive in a massive wave at 8:15 AM, while a tech company on the 30th floor arrives gradually starting at 9:00 AM.

During the morning rush hour, known in the industry as the "up-peak" period, this predictive AI shifts the elevators from a reactive state to a proactive one. Before the 8:15 AM surge even walks through the front doors, the AI automatically commands empty elevator cabs from upper floors to deadhead (travel empty at maximum speed) down to the main lobby. The cabs wait with their doors open, pre-assigned to the 60th-floor zone.

Furthermore, AI dynamically adjusts the size of the floor clusters in real time. If an unexpected influx of passengers arrives for the middle floors, the AI will temporarily dedicate more elevator cabs to that specific zone while merging higher-floor clusters that have lower immediate demand. Once sensors and turnstile data indicate that a cab has reached its optimal weight or passenger capacity, the AI bypasses any remaining hall calls and dispatches the cab immediately to prevent overcrowding delays.

By combining the real-time spatial organization of destination-hall-call clustering with the predictive power of traffic-pattern AI, supertall skyscrapers transform morning transit. Passengers spend less time waiting in crowded lobbies, experience fewer stops during their ride, and reach their workspaces with maximum efficiency.
