How Transit Agencies Use AI and Data to Stop Bus Bunching

Public transit agencies are ending the frustrating phenomenon of bus bunching—where buses on the same route arrive in pairs—by combining real-time passenger tracking with artificial intelligence. By deploying automatic passenger counters (APCs) and analyzing smart-card tap data, transit authorities can accurately measure crowd sizes and predict delays at specific stops. This data is fed directly into headway control AI systems, which dynamically adjust bus schedules on the fly to keep vehicles evenly spaced and passengers moving efficiently.

Bus bunching typically starts when a single bus is delayed by heavy traffic or a large crowd boarding at a stop. As this lead bus falls behind schedule, more passengers accumulate at upcoming stops, causing even longer boarding delays. Meanwhile, the trailing bus faces fewer waiting passengers, allowing it to move faster and eventually catch up to the delayed bus. To break this cycle, agencies rely on a steady stream of data to switch from rigid schedules to dynamic management.

Automatic passenger counters are the first line of defense. Installed above bus doorways, these systems use infrared beams, stereoscopic cameras, or thermal sensors to count every person who steps on or off the vehicle. Because APCs track every rider regardless of how they pay, they provide dispatchers with an exact, real-time measurement of a bus's current capacity. If a bus is too full, the system knows it will take longer at each stop.

To complement APCs, agencies pull real-time information from smart-card and mobile-payment tap data. When commuters tap their cards or phones to pay a fare, the system logs the exact time and location of the transaction. While APCs measure the immediate physical load of the bus, smart-card data helps build historical profiles of passenger movement. It shows transit planners where crowds typically surge at specific times of the day, allowing systems to anticipate bottlenecks before they happen.

The data from both the APCs and the fareboxes is transmitted instantaneously to a central headway control artificial intelligence. Instead of trying to force buses to stick to a strict, predetermined timetable, headway control AI focuses on maintaining an equal distance or time gap—known as "headway"—between buses on the same route.

The AI processes the live passenger data alongside GPS locations and current traffic conditions to predict where bunching will occur in the next 15 to 30 minutes. Once a potential bunching event is detected, the AI sends automated instructions straight to the dashboard screens of the bus drivers:

  • Holding: The AI may instruct the faster, trailing bus to wait at a stop for an extra 30 to 60 seconds to rebuild the gap between it and the delayed bus ahead.
  • Speed Advisory: The system can advise drivers to slightly adjust their cruising speeds where traffic safely permits.
  • Stop Skipping: In extreme cases, if a lead bus is completely full (as verified by the APCs), the AI will instruct the driver to bypass waiting passengers and only stop for drop-offs. The empty trailing bus, which is right behind it, picks up the waiting crowd, immediately balancing the load and the spacing.

By integrating physical sensors, payment data, and predictive AI, transit agencies transform reactive bus routes into self-correcting networks. This ensures buses arrive at regular, predictable intervals rather than in frustrating pairs.
