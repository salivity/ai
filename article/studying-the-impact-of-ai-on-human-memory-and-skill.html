Studying the Impact of AI on Human Memory and Skill

Cognitive scientists are increasingly investigating how our continual reliance on artificial intelligence triggers cognitive offloading and leads to the atrophy of inherent human skills. To understand this phenomenon, researchers utilize a combination of longitudinal behavioral tracking, advanced neuroimaging techniques, and controlled laboratory experiments. This article provides a straightforward examination of the exact methodologies scientists use to measure how outsourcing mental tasks to AI alters brain activity, diminishes memory retention, and degrades problem-solving abilities over time.

Controlled Behavioral Experiments

To measure cognitive offloading—the act of using external tools to reduce mental effort—scientists rely heavily on controlled A/B testing environments. Researchers divide participants into groups, assigning them identical complex tasks such as coding, essay writing, route navigation, or mathematical problem-solving.

One group uses AI assistance, while the control group relies strictly on their own cognition. After several sessions, the AI tools are abruptly removed from the assisted group. Scientists then measure the drop in performance, error rates, and the time required to complete the tasks unassisted. A sharp decline in the formerly AI-assisted group's performance compared to the control group provides direct behavioral evidence of skill atrophy.

Neuroimaging and Brain Activity

Behavioral tests only show what happens on the outside; neuroimaging reveals the biological mechanics of skill degradation inside the brain. Scientists use tools like functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) and Electroencephalography (EEG) to monitor brain activity while individuals interact with AI.

A prime focus of these scans is the hippocampus, the brain region critical for memory consolidation and spatial navigation, alongside the prefrontal cortex, which governs executive functioning and decision-making. When a person relies on GPS (a foundational form of AI) to navigate, fMRI scans consistently show reduced blood flow and neural firing in the hippocampus compared to when they navigate using mental maps. By conducting these scans over months or years, scientists can observe reduced neuroplasticity and physical shrinkage in grey matter volume within the underused regions of the brain.

Longitudinal Studies in the Workplace

Because true skill atrophy occurs gradually, cognitive scientists deploy longitudinal studies to track individuals over extended periods. Researchers frequently partner with corporate or academic institutions to monitor workers and students who integrate generative AI into their daily routines.

Every few months, participants undergo standardized cognitive assessments measuring working memory, critical thinking, and domain-specific knowledge. By correlating the frequency of an individual's AI usage with their assessment scores over a multi-year period, researchers can isolate the rate at which specific cognitive abilities—such as mental math, syntax recall, or analytical synthesis—deteriorate due to disuse.

Measuring Transactive Memory Shifts

Scientists also study how AI alters "transactive memory," a psychological mechanism where humans forget specific information itself, choosing instead to remember where to find it. Historically studied in the context of search engines, this methodology has been adapted for AI.

In these studies, participants are fed trivia or complex instructions and told either that the AI will save the information or that the AI will erase it. Subsequent memory tests reveal that participants who believe the AI will retain the knowledge perform significantly worse at recalling the information themselves. Through eye-tracking software and reaction-time tests, researchers quantify how quickly the human brain decides to abandon the effort of encoding a memory the moment it knows an AI system is present to bear the cognitive load.
