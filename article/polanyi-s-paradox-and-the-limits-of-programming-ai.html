Polanyi's Paradox and the Limits of Programming AI

This article explores Polanyi's paradox, the philosophical concept that humans know much more than they can verbally explain, and how this fundamental limitation impacts the development of artificial intelligence. We will examine the difference between tacit and explicit knowledge, why traditional rule-based programming failed to replicate basic human intuition, and how modern machine learning techniques ultimately bypassed this barrier to create advanced AI systems.

Named after the British-Hungarian philosopher and scientist Michael Polanyi, the paradox was summarized in his 1966 book, The Tacit Dimension, with the assertion that "we can know more than we can tell." Polanyi argued that a vast majority of human knowledge is tacit rather than explicit. Explicit knowledge consists of facts and procedures that can be easily written down, such as mathematical formulas or baking recipes. Tacit knowledge, conversely, is subconscious and acquired through personal experience. It is the reason humans can effortlessly recognize a familiar face, ride a bicycle, or detect sarcasm in a voice without being able to articulate the exact step-by-step mechanics behind those actions.

For decades, Polanyi's paradox served as the greatest roadblock in the development of artificial intelligence. Early AI development, often called "Good Old-Fashioned AI" (GOFAI), relied on symbolic, rule-based programming. Engineers attempted to create intelligent software by interviewing human experts and translating their knowledge into explicit lines of code using "if-then" statements. While this approach worked well for tasks with strict, definable rules like chess or tax calculation, it failed miserably at tasks humans find effortless. Programmers could not write code to help a computer distinguish between a dog and a cat because humans themselves cannot explain the exact geometric and visual rules their brains use to tell the animals apart.

This limitation reveals the core of how Polanyi's paradox impacts computer science: if humans cannot articulate the rules of how they perceive and navigate the world, they cannot write software code to instruct a machine to do the same. Physical coordination, natural language comprehension, and visual perception all rely heavily on tacit knowledge. When engineers tried to hard-code these abilities, the resulting systems were brittle and easily confused by the unpredictability of the real world.

The modern AI revolution only occurred when computer scientists stopped trying to fight Polanyi’s paradox and instead found a way around it. Rather than forcing humans to explain their tacit knowledge to computers, researchers developed machine learning and deep neural networks. Instead of being programmed with explicit rules, a machine learning model is fed massive amounts of data and programmed to find its own patterns.

To teach a modern AI to recognize a cat, engineers no longer try to write code describing pointed ears and whiskers. Instead, they show the neural network millions of labeled photos of cats and non-cats. The AI builds its own internal, mathematical understanding of what a cat looks like through trial and error. In essence, machine learning allows computers to acquire their own version of tacit knowledge through statistical experience rather than human instruction.

Ironically, overcoming Polanyi's paradox has created a new challenge in artificial intelligence known as the "black box" problem. Because modern AI systems learn implicitly from data rather than explicit human code, they often cannot explain their decision-making processes to us. Just as a human cannot fully explain how they recognize a face, an advanced neural network cannot easily articulate the billions of mathematical weights it used to arrive at a specific conclusion. Polanyi's paradox has shifted from being a barrier to creating AI to a mirror reflecting how both human and artificial minds process the unexplainable complexities of the world.
