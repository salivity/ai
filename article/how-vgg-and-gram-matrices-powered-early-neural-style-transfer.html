How VGG and Gram Matrices Powered Early Neural Style Transfer

This article provides a clear breakdown of how early neural style transfer algorithms revolutionized digital art by separating an image's structural content from its artistic style. By utilizing the pre-trained VGG convolutional neural network, researchers discovered that deep layers capture concrete objects and layouts, while mathematical constructs called Gram matrices can extract textures, colors, and brushwork across various layers by eliminating spatial data. Finally, we explore how the algorithm merges these two isolated elements to generate a brand-new image that renders the content of a photograph in the signature style of a famous painting.

To understand early neural style transfer, you first need to understand the network behind it: the VGG network. Developed by the Visual Geometry Group at Oxford, VGG is a convolutional neural network originally trained for object recognition. When an image is fed into VGG, it passes through sequential layers of digital filters. The early layers detect simple features like colors, edges, and basic textures. As the image data moves deeper into the network, the layers combine these simple features to recognize complex shapes, objects, and the overall spatial arrangement of the scene.

Extracting the content of an image using VGG is straightforward. Because the deeper convolutional layers of the network care only about high-level objects and their locations—ignoring specific pixel colors or surface textures—the algorithm simply feeds a "content image" into the VGG network and records the raw feature activations at one of these deep layers. This recorded data acts as a structural blueprint of the image, preserving where objects are located while discarding how they are textured.

Separating artistic brushwork and style from that content requires a different approach, which is where Gram matrices come in. A style is not defined by where a brushstroke is located on a canvas, but rather by how different visual elements—like specific colors, edges, and textures—appear together throughout the artwork. To capture this, the algorithm feeds a "style image" into the VGG network and looks at multiple layers, from shallow to deep, to capture both fine-grained brush textures and larger artistic motifs.

At each selected layer, the algorithm calculates a Gram matrix. Mathematically, a Gram matrix is created by taking the dot product of the vectorized feature maps within a specific layer. In simple terms, it measures the correlation between different filters. For example, if one filter detects vertical wavy lines and another detects the color yellow, the Gram matrix records how often wavy lines and yellow appear together in the painting.

The magic of the Gram matrix lies in what it leaves behind. By multiplying the feature maps together and summing them up across the entire height and width of the layer, the Gram matrix completely averages out and destroys all spatial information. The network no longer knows where the yellow wavy lines are located; it only knows that they are a dominant statistical texture throughout the image. This mathematical flattening successfully isolates the artist's brushwork and color palette from the actual subject matter of the painting.

With the content blueprint extracted from the deep layers of one image and the style correlations captured via Gram matrices from another, the algorithm begins the generation process. It starts with a blank canvas made of random white noise and feeds it through the VGG network. The algorithm then calculates two errors: the content loss (how much the canvas's deep layers differ from the content image's deep layers) and the style loss (how much the canvas's Gram matrices differ from the style image's Gram matrices).

Using an optimization process called gradient descent, the algorithm iteratively tweaks the pixel values of the noisy canvas to minimize both errors simultaneously. Step by step, the random noise transforms, aligning its structural layout with the content photograph while adjusting its local textures and colors to match the statistical correlations of the Gram matrices, ultimately achieving a seamless blend of content and artistic brushwork.
