Trading Compute for AI Alignment with Best-of-N Sampling

This article explores how Best-of-N rejection sampling, paired with a reward model, trades inference-time compute for superior alignment quality in artificial intelligence deployments. Instead of relying solely on resource-heavy post-training methods to ensure an AI behaves safely and accurately, developers can generate multiple candidate responses at runtime and use a dedicated scoring model to select the highest-quality output. While this approach significantly boosts response accuracy, safety, and helpfulness, it introduces direct trade-offs regarding computational cost, latency, and reward hacking, which engineers must balance during production.

Understanding Best-of-N and Reward Models

Best-of-N (BoN) is a straightforward inference-time algorithm. When a user submits a prompt, the language model generates N distinct responses in parallel or sequence, rather than just a single output.

Once these N candidates are generated, they are passed to a Reward Model (RM). The RM is a separate neural network trained on human preference data to evaluate and score text based on specific alignment criteria, such as helpfulness, truthfulness, and harmlessness. The system rejects the lower-scoring outputs and returns only the single highest-scoring response to the user.

The Compute-for-Quality Trade-off

Historically, improving alignment meant investing heavily in training-time compute through techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). Best-of-N shifts this burden to inference-time compute.

As the value of N increases, the probability of generating a response that closely aligns with human preferences also increases. The system effectively searches a broader space of the model's capabilities. This shift entails two specific computational costs:

  1. Generation Compute: Generating N responses requires roughly N times the memory bandwidth and floating-point operations (FLOPs) compared to a standard single-stream generation.
  2. Evaluation Compute: The reward model must process and score all N candidates, adding an extra layer of compute overhead before the final answer is delivered.

In return for paying this higher compute tax per query, developers achieve immediate alignment gains without altering the underlying weights of the base model.

Diminishing Returns and Reward Hacking

The relationship between inference compute and alignment quality is not infinitely linear. Initially, increasing N from 1 to 4, 8, or 16 yields dramatic improvements in response quality. However, as N grows larger, the gains begin to plateau.

More importantly, pushing N to extreme numbers introduces the risk of "reward hacking" (or over-optimization). Because the reward model is an imperfect proxy for actual human preferences, generating thousands of candidates increases the likelihood that the base model will stumble upon an output that exploits flaws or biases in the RM. The RM might assign a maximum score to a response that is overly verbose, sycophantic, or formatting-heavy, resulting in a degradation of actual alignment quality despite massive compute expenditure.

Practical Deployment Considerations

When deploying Best-of-N in production environments, engineering teams must balance alignment needs against operational constraints:

  • Latency vs. Throughput: By running the N generations in parallel on modern GPUs, the time-to-first-token (latency) remains relatively stable, though the final step of scoring adds a slight delay. However, overall system throughput drops significantly because each user query consumes a much larger slice of available GPU memory.
  • Selective Application: Because multiplying inference compute for every query is financially prohibitive, deployments often route only high-stakes, complex, or sensitive prompts through the Best-of-N pipeline. Simple queries bypass the process and use standard greedy decoding (N=1).
  • Baseline Equivalence: A smaller, cheaper base model running Best-of-N at inference can often match or exceed the alignment quality of a massive, expensive model running a single generation. This allows organizations to dynamically scale their compute budget based on real-time demand rather than upfront training costs.
