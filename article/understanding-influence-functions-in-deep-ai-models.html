Understanding Influence Functions in Deep AI Models

Influence functions provide a mathematical shortcut to estimate how much a deep artificial intelligence model's predictions would change if a single training example were removed, entirely bypassing the need for expensive retraining. This article explores the core mechanics of influence functions, detailing how they use upweighting, second-order Taylor approximations, the Hessian matrix, and the chain rule to trace a model's test loss back to individual training data points. By understanding these mechanisms, developers can efficiently debug datasets, explain model behaviors, and identify mislabeled training examples.

The Core Problem: Leave-One-Out Retraining

To truly know the impact of a single training example on a deep learning model, you would ideally remove that data point, retrain the entire model from scratch, and observe the change in the test loss. This is known as Leave-One-Out (LOO) retraining. For modern deep AI models with millions or billions of parameters, retraining the network even once is computationally prohibitive; doing it for every single training example is impossible. Influence functions solve this by approximating the LOO effect using calculus.

Upweighting Instead of Removing

Instead of abruptly deleting a training example—which is a discrete action that calculus cannot easily handle—influence functions reframe the problem as a continuous one. They assign a tiny weight, denoted as \(\epsilon\) (epsilon), to a specific training example \(z\).

If \(\epsilon = 0\), the model trains normally. If \(\epsilon = -\frac{1}{n}\) (where \(n\) is the total number of training examples), it is mathematically equivalent to removing that example from the dataset. Influence functions calculate the rate of change in the model's parameters as \(\epsilon\) moves slightly away from zero.

Parameter Shift via Taylor Approximation

When the weight \(\epsilon\) of a training example is changed, the optimal parameters (weights and biases) of the model shift to minimize the new total training loss. Because the change in weight is infinitesimally small, influence functions assume the new optimal parameters are very close to the original parameters.

To find where these new parameters settle without actually running gradient descent, the method applies a second-order Taylor expansion (quadratic approximation) to the loss function around the original optimal parameters. This local approximation treats the complex, high-dimensional loss landscape immediately surrounding the current model state as a smooth bowl.

The Role of the Hessian Matrix

Calculating how the parameters shift within this local "bowl" requires two pieces of mathematical information regarding the specific training point \(z\):

  1. The Gradient: The first derivative of the loss on the training example \(z\). This shows the direction the example is pushing the model's parameters.
  2. The Inverse Hessian Matrix: The Hessian is a square matrix of second-order partial derivatives of the total training loss. It describes the local curvature of the loss landscape.

The shift in the model's parameters is approximated by multiplying the inverse of the Hessian matrix by the gradient of the training example:

\[\text{Parameter Change} \approx -H_{\theta}^{-1} \nabla_{\theta} L(z, \theta)\]

If the loss landscape is very flat (low curvature) in a certain direction, removing the data point will cause the parameters to shift significantly. If the landscape is very steep (high curvature), the parameters are tightly constrained by other data points and will barely move.

Connecting Parameter Shift to Test Loss

Once the influence function approximates how the model's internal parameters will change upon removing the training example, the final step is to see how those new parameters affect a specific test example, \(z_{\text{test}}\).

Using the chain rule of calculus, the predicted change in the parameters is multiplied by the gradient of the loss of the test example. The complete formula for the influence of removing training point \(z\) on the loss at test point \(z_{\text{test}}\) is:

\[\text{Influence}(z, z_{\text{test}}) \approx -\frac{1}{n} \nabla_{\theta} L(z_{\text{test}}, \theta)^T H_{\theta}^{-1} \nabla_{\theta} L(z, \theta)\]

In plain terms, this equation asks two questions: How much does the training point change the model's weights, and how sensitive is the test point's loss to those specific weight changes?

Computational Tricks for Deep Learning

In deep AI models, the Hessian matrix is massive. A model with 10 million parameters results in a Hessian with 100 trillion entries, making it impossible to store in memory or invert directly. To make influence functions work in practice, algorithms do not compute the actual Hessian. Instead, they use implicit Hessian-vector products (HVPs) combined with optimization techniques like Conjugate Gradients or Stochastic Estimation (LiSSA). These methods approximate the result of \(H_{\theta}^{-1}v\) iteratively using only standard backpropagation steps, keeping computation fast and memory-efficient.

Limitations in Non-Convex Models

Originally developed for simple, convex statistical models, influence functions face theoretical challenges in deep learning. Deep neural networks have non-convex loss landscapes with many local minima, meaning the Hessian is not always invertible. To bypass this, practitioners add a small "damping" term to the Hessian to force invertibility and ensure the quadratic approximation remains stable, allowing influence functions to provide accurate, actionable estimates even in complex AI architectures.
