AI and the Rubin Observatory: Tracking 10M Nightly Alerts

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory is set to revolutionize astronomy by capturing the entire visible southern sky every few nights, generating an unprecedented ten million transient alerts each evening. To manage this massive influx of data, the observatory relies on real-time artificial intelligence alert brokers and advanced tracklet-linking algorithms. This article explores how these AI systems instantly filter millions of cosmic events, separate stationary phenomena from moving objects, and calculate the orbits of potentially hazardous asteroids to safeguard Earth.

The core mission of the observatory, the Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST), utilizes a 3,200-megapixel camera to take rapid-fire exposures of the deep sky. Whenever the telescope takes a new image, automated software compares it against a baseline template of the same sky region. Any change in brightness or position—such as an exploding supernova, a flaring variable star, or a moving asteroid—triggers a data packet known as a transient alert within sixty seconds of the shutter closing.

Processing ten million alerts every night is far beyond human capability. Instead of streaming raw data directly to astronomers, the observatory feeds these alerts into a global network of software systems called community alert brokers. Systems like ANTARES, Fink, ALeRCE, and Lasair act as intelligent data clearinghouses. They ingest the massive data stream in real time, cross-reference the coordinates with existing astronomical catalogs, and prepare the data for classification.

Artificial intelligence and machine learning form the backbone of these alert brokers. Using deep learning models, such as convolutional neural networks and random forests, the brokers analyze the light curves and image stamps included in each alert. Within milliseconds, the AI filters out instrumental noise, satellite glints, and cosmic ray hits. It then categorizes genuine astronomical events, flagging rare phenomena for immediate follow-up by other telescopes around the world.

While stationary transients remain in the same spot across multiple images, solar system objects like asteroids move across the sky. Because they move, an asteroid triggers a new alert at a different location every time the telescope photographs that region. The greatest computational challenge is realizing that several separate alerts captured over hours or days actually belong to the same moving rock, a process known as linking.

To solve this, the observatory utilizes specialized software called the Solar System Processing pipeline, powered by an algorithm known as HelioLinC3D. When the AI brokers identify uncatalogued points of light that appear only once in a specific spot, those detections are routed into this pipeline. The software groups pairs of detections taken during a single night into short paths called tracklets.

Linking these tracklets across multiple nights traditionally required immense computing power because millions of false combinations are possible. HelioLinC3D overcomes this bottleneck by assuming a range of possible distances and velocities relative to the Sun, transforming the data into a three-dimensional space where detections of the same asteroid naturally cluster together. This allows the system to identify a valid orbit using fewer observations per night than older methods required.

Once the software successfully links three nights of tracklets into a coherent orbital path, the object is validated as a genuine discovery. The trajectory is then projected forward to determine if its path intersects with Earth's orbit. Any object that comes within a specific proximity to Earth and exceeds a certain brightness threshold is immediately flagged as a Potentially Hazardous Asteroid (PHA).

Finally, these linked orbits are transmitted directly to the International Astronomical Union’s Minor Planet Center. From there, planetary defense networks at NASA and the European Space Agency take over to refine the orbit and assess impact risks. By combining the Rubin Observatory's massive light-gathering power with real-time AI brokers and advanced linking algorithms, humanity will increase its catalog of known near-Earth asteroids tenfold, providing crucial decades of advance warning against potential cosmic impacts.
