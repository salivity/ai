AI Anti-Cheat in Chess, Go, and Esports

This article explores how modern competitive integrity teams in chess, Go, and esports detect human players secretly using artificial intelligence engines to cheat. By combining statistical algorithms like move-entropy and centipawn loss analysis with behavioral biometrics such as mouse-micro-adjustment tracking, anti-cheat systems can accurately distinguish between human intuition and machine calculation. This overview covers the exact metrics and machine learning models used to protect fair play in digital competitions.

Centipawn Loss Analysis in Chess

In chess, engines evaluate board positions using a unit called a centipawn, which equals one-hundredth of a pawn's value. When a player makes a move, anti-cheat systems compare that choice to the top move recommended by engines like Stockfish. The difference in value between the engine's best move and the player's actual move is the Centipawn Loss (CPL).

Human players, including Grandmasters, naturally make suboptimal moves under time pressure or in complex positions, resulting in fluctuating Average Centipawn Loss (ACPL). Cheaters consulting an engine consistently register abnormally low ACPL scores. However, because low ACPL can occasionally happen in simple games, integrity teams combine it with move-entropy analysis to confirm cheating.

Move-Entropy in Chess and Go

Move-entropy measures the complexity and chaos of a given board state. High-entropy positions in chess and Go have multiple viable paths, making it extremely difficult for a human to calculate the absolute best move. Low-entropy positions have obvious moves, such as an automatic recapture, where a human and an engine will naturally make the same choice.

In Go, engines like KataGo evaluate moves based on win-rate percentages and point loss rather than centipawns. Anti-cheat algorithms calculate the entropy of the board and evaluate how a player performs specifically in high-entropy states. If a player consistently finds the top engine move during chaotic, high-entropy moments where human intuition typically fails, the system flags the account. It is statistically impossible for a human to match an engine's exact choices only when the board is at its most complicated.

Mouse-Micro-Adjustment AI in Esports

While board games rely heavily on decision statistics, real-time esports and online strategy platforms also use behavioral biometrics, specifically mouse-micro-adjustment artificial intelligence. Human motor control is imperfect. When a human moves a mouse cursor toward a target—whether clicking a chess piece or aiming at an opponent in a shooter—the movement follows a curved path with continuous, microscopic corrections in speed and trajectory.

Anti-cheat AI models are trained on millions of hours of human mouse data to establish a baseline of natural biomechanics. This data detects two types of cheating:

  1. Automated Aiming (Esports): Aimbots move the cursor in mathematically perfect straight lines or use artificial smoothing algorithms. Mouse-micro-adjustment AI detects the absence of natural human jitter, over-correction, and reaction delay.
  2. Engine Consultation (Chess and Go): When a player consults a second screen, phone, or overlay to read an engine's move, their mouse behavior changes. The AI detects robotic cadence, unusual pauses before simple moves, lack of "hovering" over alternative pieces, and sudden shifts in cursor velocity that indicate the player is simply inputting commands rather than thinking through the interface.

Combining Metrics for Definitive Proof

No single metric is used to ban a player in high-stakes environments. A lucky human might play a low-ACPL game, and a player with a high-end mouse might have smooth cursor movement. Integrity teams feed all these variables into an overarching machine learning classifier.

When a player exhibits near-zero centipawn or point loss during high-entropy board states, alongside unnatural mouse-micro-adjustments and robotic time-usage patterns, the probability of human play drops to near zero. This multi-layered approach allows anti-cheat teams to catch sophisticated cheaters who only consult engines on a few critical moves per game.
