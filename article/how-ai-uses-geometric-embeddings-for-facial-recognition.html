How AI Uses Geometric Embeddings for Facial Recognition

Facial recognition artificial intelligence verifies identity by translating the physical structure of a human face into a mathematical representation known as a geometric embedding. This article explores how deep learning models extract these unique facial vectors, measure mathematical distances to confirm identity, and utilize specialized training techniques to remain accurate despite changes in lighting and natural aging.

The Extraction of Geometric Embeddings

Before an AI can measure a face, it must detect and align it. Once a face is located within an image, the AI normalizes the image by rotating and scaling it so that key landmarks—such as the corners of the eyes and the tip of the nose—are in standard positions.

Next, the aligned image is fed into a Deep Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). Instead of simply measuring pixel values, the CNN passes the image through multiple hidden layers to extract high-level spatial features, such as the distance between the eyes, the contour of the jawline, and the depth of the eye sockets. The network compresses this structural data into a geometric embedding: a fixed-length array of numbers (often 128 to 512 dimensions) that plots the face as a single point in a high-dimensional mathematical space.

Measuring Distance to Verify Identity

To verify an identity, the AI compares the newly generated embedding from a live camera feed against a stored reference embedding. Because these embeddings exist as coordinates in a multi-dimensional space, the AI uses geometry to measure how close the two points are to one another.

The two primary methods for this are:

  • Euclidean Distance: This measures the straight-line distance between two points in the embedding space. If the distance is smaller than a predetermined threshold, the faces are considered a match.
  • Cosine Similarity: This measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors projected from the origin. Instead of looking at the magnitude of the vectors, it looks at their orientation. An angle close to zero degrees indicates that the two embeddings share the same geometric structure, confirming the identity.

Overcoming Lighting Variations

Lighting changes drastically alter the pixel values of an image, casting shadows that can obscure facial features. AI overcomes this by ignoring surface-level pixel intensity and focusing on invariant structural topology.

During the preprocessing phase, algorithms apply illumination normalization, such as histogram equalization, to balance contrast. More importantly, the CNN is trained using data augmentation. During training, the AI is shown millions of images of the same individuals under extreme lighting conditions—backlit, dimly lit, and harshly lit from the side. Using loss functions like Triplet Loss or ArcFace, the network is penalized if it places two differently lit photos of the same person far apart in the embedding space. Consequently, the AI learns to discard lighting data altogether, anchoring the embedding strictly to bone structure and feature ratios.

Adapting to the Aging Process

As humans age, skin loses elasticity, wrinkles form, and facial volume shifts. However, the underlying craniofacial skeleton remains largely unchanged in adulthood.

Modern facial recognition AI maintains accuracy across decades by utilizing Age-Invariant Face Recognition (AIFR) techniques. Neural networks are trained on longitudinal datasets containing photos of the same individuals taken across spans of 10 to 30 years. Through attention mechanisms, the AI learns to assign higher mathematical weight to permanent skeletal landmarks—like the orbital bones, nasal bridge, and interpupillary distance—while assigning near-zero weight to transient textures like skin smoothness, hairlines, and adipose tissue.

In advanced systems, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) are also used to digitally "age" or "de-age" a face before extracting the embedding, ensuring that the geometric vector represents the core, unchanging identity of the person.
