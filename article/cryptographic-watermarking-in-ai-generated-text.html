Cryptographic Watermarking in AI-Generated Text

Cryptographic watermarking is a sophisticated technique used to identify text generated by artificial intelligence by embedding invisible, mathematically verifiable signals directly into the word choices of a large language model. This article provides a comprehensive breakdown of how this technology functions, starting with the inner workings of AI token generation and moving through the pseudorandom partitioning of vocabulary using secret cryptographic keys. Additionally, it explores how these hidden statistical patterns are mathematically detected without altering the readability of the text, as well as the current limitations and vulnerabilities of watermarking systems.

How Large Language Models Generate Text

To understand cryptographic watermarking, one must first understand how Large Language Models (LLMs) write. LLMs do not think in full sentences; they generate text one "token" (a word or part of a word) at a time.

When you give an AI a prompt, it processes the input and calculates a probability score for every single token in its massive vocabulary to determine what should come next. For example, in the sentence "The sky is...", the token "blue" might have a 70% probability, "clear" a 15% probability, and "gray" a 10% probability. Normally, the AI selects the next token by sampling from these top probable choices, introducing a slight randomness so the text does not sound robotic. Watermarking steps into this exact moment of selection.

The Role of the Cryptographic Key

Cryptographic watermarking manipulates the token selection process before the AI makes its final choice. It achieves this using a secret cryptographic key held by the AI developer and a mathematical tool called a hash function.

Right before the AI selects the next token, the watermarking algorithm takes the previously generated token and combines it with the secret cryptographic key. It feeds this combination into the hash function, which outputs a pseudorandom number, known as a "seed." Because this seed is generated using a cryptographic hash, it appears completely random to anyone without the secret key, yet it is entirely deterministic and repeatable for anyone who possesses the key.

Green Lists, Red Lists, and Probability Biasing

The pseudorandom seed generated in the previous step is used to temporarily divide the AI's entire vocabulary into two distinct groups for that specific step in the sentence: a "green list" (allowed tokens) and a "red list" (restricted tokens).

In a strict watermarking system, the AI is forced to choose its next word exclusively from the green list. However, doing this rigidly can ruin the quality of the text, especially if a necessary word (like a specific name or grammar piece) lands on the red list.

To maintain natural readability, modern cryptographic watermarking uses a "soft" approach. Instead of banning red-list words entirely, the algorithm slightly increases the probability scores of the words on the green list before the AI makes its final selection. If a red-list word is overwhelmingly the best fit for the sentence, its naturally high probability will override the bias, and the AI will still use it. However, when the AI has several equally good synonyms to choose from, the added mathematical nudge ensures it will almost always pick the synonym on the green list. Once the token is chosen, the process repeats for the next word, generating a new seed and a new green list.

Detecting the Invisible Signal

To a human reader, the resulting text looks completely normal. There are no hidden metadata tags, strange spellings, or invisible characters that can be stripped away by copying and pasting the text into a plain document. The watermark is the text itself.

Detection requires the secret cryptographic key. A detection algorithm reads the suspect text token by token. Using the preceding word and the secret key, the detector recalculates the exact pseudorandom seed that would have been used at each step, reconstructing the green and red lists for every word in the document.

The detector then counts how many words in the text fall onto the green lists. If a human wrote the text, their word choices would align with the green lists purely by chance—roughly 50% of the time. If the watermarked AI wrote the text, a statistically unnatural number of words—perhaps 70% or 80%—will belong to the green lists. Using a statistical measurement called a z-score, the detector calculates the probability that this alignment happened by random chance. As the text gets longer, the mathematical certainty that it was generated by the AI approaches 100%.

Limitations and Vulnerabilities

While cryptographic watermarking is a powerful tool for provenance, it is not foolproof. Its effectiveness relies heavily on two factors: text length and "entropy" (randomness in word choice).

Short texts, such as a single tweet, do not contain enough tokens to build a statistically significant pattern. Similarly, low-entropy texts—such as computer code, math equations, or factual recitations where there is only one correct way to write the output—leave the AI no room to choose green-list synonyms, effectively preventing the watermark from being embedded.

Furthermore, watermarks are vulnerable to "spoofing" or removal attacks. If a user takes watermarked AI text and runs it through a secondary AI specifically instructed to paraphrase every sentence, or manually swaps out every third word with a synonym, the mathematical chain of green-list tokens is broken, and the statistical signal degrades until it is no longer detectable.
