AI and Cross-Lingual Alignment in Deciphering Linear A

Computational linguists are revolutionizing archaeology by applying artificial intelligence and cross-lingual embedding alignment to decode undeciphered ancient scripts like Linear A. By converting characters and words into multidimensional mathematical vectors, AI models can map the structural relationships of an unknown script and compare them against known historical languages. This article explores the mechanics of vector space alignment, the algorithmic rules used to match ancient symbols to spoken tongues, and the specific data limitations researchers face when attempting to crack the code of Minoan Linear A.

In modern natural language processing, words are represented as embeddings—numerical vectors placed in a multidimensional space. Words that appear in similar contexts are positioned closer together in this space. For example, in any given language, the words for "king" and "queen" will have a similar geometric relationship to "man" and "woman." Computational linguists use this principle to map undeciphered scripts. Even without knowing what Linear A symbols mean, AI can analyze the surviving texts to determine how frequently symbols appear together, creating a geometric map of the unknown language's structure.

Once the AI generates a vector space for the undeciphered script, it uses cross-lingual embedding alignment to compare this map with the vector spaces of known ancient languages, such as Mycenaean Greek, Luwian, or Semitic dialects. The AI attempts to rotate and align the two geometric maps until the structures overlap. If the underlying language of the undeciphered script is related to the known language, the clusters of vectors will match up, allowing researchers to translate symbols based on their position in the aligned space.

Because ancient scripts rarely have massive datasets, standard AI translation models often fail. To overcome this, researchers program the AI with historical and linguistic constraints. The algorithms factor in the phonological rules of how sounds change over centuries. They also focus heavily on cognates—words that share a common ancestral root—and proper nouns, such as the names of cities, kings, and deities. Because proper nouns tend to sound similar across different languages, they act as anchor points to lock the vector spaces into the correct alignment.

Applying this technology to Linear A—the script of the ancient Minoan civilization—presents unique challenges. Researchers first use a related, deciphered script called Linear B (which represents an early form of Greek) to guess the phonetic values of Linear A symbols, as the two scripts share many visual characters. The AI then takes these phonetic guesses and searches for structural matches across dozens of known Mediterranean and Near Eastern languages, effectively testing thousands of linguistic hypotheses in a fraction of the time it would take a human philologist.

Despite these advancements, Linear A remains undeciphered due to severe data scarcity. There are only about 1,400 surviving Linear A inscriptions, most of which are short, fragmented accounting records rather than long narratives. AI typically requires vast amounts of text to build accurate vector spaces. Furthermore, if Linear A is a language isolate—meaning it has no known linguistic relatives—cross-lingual alignment cannot work because there is no matching language map to align it with.

While AI has not yet produced a definitive translation of Linear A, it has successfully automated the process of identifying structural patterns, word boundaries, and administrative formulas within the text. By narrowing down the possible linguistic families and phonetic values, cross-lingual embedding alignment provides human epigraphers with a powerful mathematical tool that brings them closer to reading the lost voices of the ancient world.
