3D Gaussian Splatting vs. NeRFs: Speeding Up AI Graphics

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has revolutionized artificial intelligence graphics by enabling high-fidelity, real-time rendering that significantly outpaces traditional Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs). This article explores the core architectural differences between these two technologies, detailing how 3DGS bypasses the heavy computational bottlenecks of neural networks by using explicit 3D representations and a tile-based rasterization pipeline to achieve smooth frame rates.

To understand why 3D Gaussian Splatting is so much faster, it is necessary to look at the limitations of Neural Radiance Fields. NeRFs represent a 3D scene implicitly inside a neural network, specifically a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). To render a single pixel, a NeRF must shoot a virtual ray into the scene, sample hundreds of points along that ray, and query the neural network at every single point to predict color and density. This process, known as volumetric ray marching, requires millions of neural network evaluations per frame, making real-time rendering nearly impossible without massive hardware compromises.

3D Gaussian Splatting eliminates the neural network from the rendering phase entirely. Instead of an implicit mathematical function, 3DGS uses an explicit representation. It begins by taking a sparse point cloud generated from camera images and converts these points into millions of 3D Gaussians. You can think of a 3D Gaussian as a soft, semi-transparent ellipsoid floating in space. Each Gaussian stores specific attributes: its 3D position, a covariance matrix (which dictates its size, shape, and rotation), opacity, and color data via spherical harmonics (which allows lighting and reflections to change based on the viewing angle).

The primary reason 3DGS achieves real-time speeds is that it swaps slow ray marching for fast rasterization. Rasterization is the exact same technique modern video game engines use to project 3D polygons onto a 2D screen, and graphics processing units (GPUs) have been specifically optimized to do this at lightning speeds for decades. When rendering a frame, 3DGS projects the 3D Gaussians onto a 2D image plane, effectively "splatting" them onto the screen like paintballs.

To further maximize rendering speeds, 3DGS utilizes a custom, tile-based rasterizer. The system divides the 2D screen into small 16x16 pixel tiles. It then quickly determines which Gaussians overlap with which tiles and sorts them by depth, from front to back, only once per frame. Each tile is then processed in parallel by the GPU. Because the Gaussians are sorted from front to back, the renderer blends their colors and opacities until a pixel becomes completely opaque. Once full opacity is reached, the system immediately stops processing any remaining Gaussians behind it. This early termination saves immense amounts of computational power by ignoring data that the viewer cannot see.

By removing the need to query a neural network millions of times per second and avoiding the evaluation of empty space—a major flaw in NeRF's ray marching—3D Gaussian Splatting drastically reduces the workload on the GPU. While NeRFs often struggle to generate a few frames per second at high resolutions, 3DGS easily achieves over 100 frames per second at 1080p resolution, making it an ideal solution for virtual reality, gaming, and real-time visual effects.
