Why Tree-Based Models Beat Deep Learning on Tabular Data

Artificial intelligence benchmarks consistently show that while deep learning dominates computer vision and natural language processing, traditional tree-based models like XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost remain superior for tabular data. This article explores the core reasons behind this phenomenon, examining how decision trees naturally handle the irregular patterns, uninformative features, and heterogeneous structures typical of tabular datasets, whereas neural networks struggle due to their bias toward smooth, continuous functions and spatial invariance.

1. Handling Irregular Target Functions

Deep learning models, particularly standard Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs), are biased toward learning overly smooth solutions. They excel at finding continuous, gradual patterns in data. Tabular data, however, often contains irregular, sharp decision boundaries where a tiny change in a single feature (like age or credit score) abruptly changes the outcome. Tree-based models make hard, axis-aligned splits, allowing them to easily learn these non-smooth, step-like functions without extensive training.

2. Uninformative and Correlated Features

Tabular datasets frequently contain irrelevant or noisy columns. Neural networks mix all input features in their first layer through matrix multiplication. Consequently, uninformative features can easily corrupt the learning signal, causing the network to overfit. Tree-based models evaluate features individually at each split node. If a feature is useless, the algorithm simply ignores it and chooses a more informative feature, making trees inherently robust to noise.

3. Lack of Spatial or Temporal Invariance

Deep learning thrives on images, audio, and text because the data is homogeneous—pixels are always just pixels, and nearby pixels are strongly related. Neural networks use this structure to share weights and build hierarchical representations. Tabular data is heterogeneous; Column A might be a categorical text value, Column B a financial figure, and Column C a date. The order of columns in a spreadsheet is completely arbitrary. Because neural networks are typically rotationally invariant (treating inputs as a unified geometric space), they lose the specific meaning attached to individual, independent features. Trees are not rotationally invariant; they treat every column as a distinct, isolated entity, preserving the natural structure of the table.

4. Preprocessing and Data Heterogeneity

Preparing tabular data for deep learning is notoriously difficult. Neural networks require numerical inputs, forcing data scientists to one-hot encode categorical variables, impute missing values, and strictly normalize numerical scales to prevent exploding gradients. Tree-based models require minimal preprocessing. They are invariant to monotonic transformations, meaning you do not need to scale or normalize the data. Furthermore, modern gradient boosting frameworks handle missing values and categorical features natively.

5. Computational Efficiency and Hyperparameter Tuning

Training deep neural networks on tabular data requires significant computational resources, typically demanding GPUs and extensive hyperparameter tuning (e.g., finding the right learning rate, architecture depth, dropout rates, and activation functions) just to achieve baseline performance. Tree-based ensembles are fast, can often be trained efficiently on standard CPUs, and usually deliver state-of-the-art results out-of-the-box with default hyperparameters.

Summary

While researchers continue to develop specialized neural architectures (like TabNet and FT-Transformer) to bridge the gap, tree-based models remain the gold standard for tabular data. Their ability to isolate features, create sharp decision boundaries, and handle raw, messy data makes them uniquely suited for the realities of structured datasets.
