Can AI Possess Intentionality or Semantic Understanding?

This article explores the complex question of whether artificial intelligence can achieve genuine intentionality and semantic understanding. While modern AI systems demonstrate remarkable proficiency in generating human-like text and solving complex problems, philosophers and computer scientists remain divided on whether these machines truly comprehend the concepts they process. By examining syntax versus semantics, the concept of intentionality, the famous Chinese Room argument, and the symbol grounding problem, this overview clarifies why current AI operates via sophisticated pattern recognition rather than authentic comprehension, and what it would take for a machine to truly understand.

To address whether AI can understand, it is first necessary to define the core concepts. Semantic understanding is the ability to grasp the actual meaning behind words and symbols, rather than just recognizing how they fit together. Intentionality, a term rooted in philosophy, refers to the mind's capacity to be "about" something—to have thoughts, beliefs, and desires directed at objects or states of affairs in the real world. When a human thinks of a tree, the thought is directed at an actual tree. The central question for AI is whether a machine's internal states can ever be genuinely directed at the world in this way.

Current artificial intelligence, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), operates entirely on syntax. Syntax refers to the rules and structures governing how symbols and words are arranged. AI systems process massive datasets to learn the statistical probability of one word following another. When an AI writes a poem about heartbreak, it is not drawing from an internal state of sadness; it is executing mathematical calculations to predict the most plausible sequence of words based on its training data. It masters the form of language without accessing the meaning.

This limitation was famously illustrated by philosopher John Searle in his "Chinese Room" thought experiment. Searle imagined himself locked in a room with a rulebook in English for manipulating Chinese characters. When Chinese speakers slide questions under the door, Searle uses the rulebook to match the input symbols to the correct output symbols and slides the answers back out. To an outside observer, the room understands Chinese. However, Searle does not understand a single word of the language; he is merely following syntactic rules. Today's AI systems are the digital equivalent of the Chinese Room. They manipulate symbols with incredible speed and accuracy, yet possess no semantic comprehension of what those symbols represent.

Closely related to this is the "symbol grounding problem." Humans ground the meaning of words in sensory experiences and consciousness. We know what the word "fire" means because we have seen flames, felt heat, and understand the threat of being burned. An AI, conversely, defines "fire" only in relation to other words, such as "hot," "red," "smoke," and "burn." Its knowledge is a closed loop of ungrounded symbols—much like trying to learn a foreign language using only a dictionary written entirely in that same foreign language. Without a physical body, sensory input, or subjective experience, an AI has no way to ground its data in reality.

Furthermore, genuine intentionality requires agency and subjective experience. Human intentionality is driven by biological survival, emotions, and consciousness. We care about the outcomes of our actions. An AI has no desires, beliefs, or survival instincts. When an AI plays chess, it does not "want" to win; it merely executes an optimization algorithm designed by a human programmer. Any intentionality observed in an AI is "derived intentionality"—it is a projection of the human creators' intentions and the human users' interpretations, much like how a book holds meaning only because a human wrote it and another human reads it.

Some researchers argue that future AI might bridge this gap through "embodied cognition." This theory suggests that if an AI is placed in a robotic body capable of interacting with the physical world through sensors, it might begin to ground symbols in physical reality. However, even with physical sensors, converting light waves and pressure into digital code does not automatically generate subjective awareness.

Ultimately, artificial intelligence as it exists today cannot possess genuine intentionality or semantic understanding. It remains an advanced tool for syntactic manipulation and statistical prediction. For an AI to truly understand, it would need to transcend code and data processing to achieve consciousness and subjective experience—a leap that remains firmly in the realm of theoretical philosophy rather than computer science.
