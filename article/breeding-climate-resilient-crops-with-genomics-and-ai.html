Breeding Climate-Resilient Crops with Genomics and AI

Plant breeders are rapidly developing drought-tolerant and heat-resilient wheat, rice, and maize cultivars by combining genomic selection with high-throughput phenotyping and artificial intelligence. This article provides an overview of how scientists read plant DNA to predict survival traits, utilize automated sensors and drones to measure physical crop stress in the field, and apply machine learning algorithms to fuse these massive datasets. By integrating these advanced technologies, breeders can bypass traditional, decades-long breeding cycles to quickly identify and produce staple crops capable of surviving extreme weather.

Genomic Selection: Predicting Resilience from DNA

Traditional plant breeding requires growing thousands of plants to maturity just to see which ones survive heat and drought. Genomic selection eliminates this waiting game by focusing on the plant's DNA before it even goes into the ground.

Breeders start by creating a "training population" of wheat, rice, or maize. They sequence the DNA of these plants using genome-wide genetic markers, known as Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs), and simultaneously test how the plants perform in dry, hot environments. Once the correlation between specific genetic patterns and stress tolerance is established, breeders no longer need to wait for a plant to mature to know its value. They can simply extract DNA from a tiny seed or seedling, calculate its Genomic Estimated Breeding Value (GEBV), and immediately select the individuals with the highest genetic potential for drought and heat resilience.

High-Throughput Phenotyping: Measuring Physical Stress at Scale

While genomics reveals a plant's potential, breeders still need to observe its physical traits—known as its phenotype—under real-world stress. Historically, this meant breeders walked through fields with clipboards, visually scoring plants. Today, High-Throughput Phenotyping (HTP) automates this process using drones, satellites, robotic field rovers, and automated greenhouses.

To breed for heat and drought tolerance, HTP systems rely on specialized imaging sensors:

  • Thermal Imaging: Measures canopy temperature. Under drought and heat stress, plants close their pores (stomata) to save water, causing them to heat up. Cooler canopy temperatures in a hot field indicate that a wheat or maize variety has deep roots still accessing water and is actively cooling itself.
  • Multispectral and Hyperspectral Sensors: Capture light wavelengths invisible to the human eye. These sensors detect subtle changes in chlorophyll content, cellular water levels, and photosynthetic efficiency in rice and other crops long before the leaves visibly wilt or turn yellow.
  • LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging): Uses lasers to create 3D models of crops. This helps breeders track growth rates, biomass accumulation, and changes in leaf angles—such as maize leaves rolling up to reduce sun exposure during a drought.

Artificial Intelligence: Connecting the Genes to the Traits

Genomic sequencing and high-throughput phenotyping generate terabytes of complex data daily. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) act as the bridge that connects this genetic data to physical field performance, alongside shifting weather variables.

Computer vision, a branch of AI, automatically processes thousands of drone images to count wheat spikes, measure rice panicles, or quantify leaf scorching in maize with pinpoint accuracy. Meanwhile, deep learning algorithms analyze the non-linear relationships between a plant’s DNA markers, its sensor-derived traits, and environmental data like soil moisture and ambient temperature.

Unlike human analysts, AI can spot microscopic patterns across millions of data points. For example, an ML model might discover that a specific combination of 50 minor genes in rice results in a slightly cooler canopy temperature during flowering, which directly protects grain yield during a heatwave.

The Impact on Wheat, Rice, and Maize

By uniting these three technologies, breeders tailor their strategies to the specific vulnerabilities of the world's top three staple crops:

  • Wheat: AI-driven phenotyping helps breeders identify wheat lines with the "stay-green" trait, where leaves remain photosynthetically active longer during terminal drought. Genomic selection then allows them to rapidly stack the genes responsible for this trait with genes that encourage deeper root architectures.
  • Rice: Rice is exceptionally sensitive to heat stress during its flowering stage, which causes sterility. Breeders use thermal cameras and AI to pinpoint varieties that flower early in the morning when temperatures are cooler (a trait called floral time-of-day), and use genomic models to breed this trait into high-yielding commercial lines.
  • Maize: Drought causes a fatal delay between pollen shedding and silk emergence in maize. High-throughput sensors monitor this timing across tens of thousands of hybrids simultaneously. AI integrates this data with genomic profiles to select parent lines that synchronize reproduction seamlessly, even under severe water deficits.

Through the synergy of genomic selection, high-throughput phenotyping, and artificial intelligence, plant breeders have reduced the time required to release a new climate-resilient cultivar from a decade to just a few years. This precision ensures global food security can keep pace with a rapidly warming planet.
