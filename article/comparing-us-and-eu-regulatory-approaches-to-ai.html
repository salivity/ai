Comparing US and EU Regulatory Approaches to AI

This article provides a direct comparison between the decentralized, sector-specific regulatory approach to artificial intelligence in the United States and the centralized, risk-based framework established by the European Union. While the EU relies on the comprehensive AI Act to enforce uniform standards across its member states, the US currently utilizes a combination of executive actions, existing federal agency mandates, and a rapidly growing patchwork of individual state laws. Below, we examine the core differences in how these two global powers govern AI development and deployment.

The European Union's Centralized Framework

The European Union governs artificial intelligence through the EU AI Act, the world's first comprehensive, horizontal legal framework for AI. This centralized approach applies uniformly across all 27 EU member states, treating AI regulation similarly to product safety legislation.

The EU AI Act categorizes AI systems into four distinct risk tiers:

  • Unacceptable Risk: AI systems that threaten fundamental rights, such as social scoring by governments or manipulative subliminal techniques, are completely banned.
  • High Risk: Systems used in critical areas like healthcare, law enforcement, critical infrastructure, and employment must undergo strict conformity assessments, maintain high data quality, and ensure human oversight before entering the market.
  • Limited Risk: AI systems that interact directly with humans, such as chatbots or deepfakes, are subject to specific transparency obligations, ensuring users know they are interacting with AI.
  • Minimal Risk: The vast majority of AI systems, such as spam filters and AI-enabled video games, face no new legal obligations under the Act.

In addition to risk tiers, the EU specifically regulates General-Purpose AI (GPAI) models, requiring foundational model providers to adhere to transparency standards, copyright laws, and systemic risk evaluations.

The United States Federal Approach

Unlike the EU, the United States has not passed comprehensive federal legislation specifically targeting artificial intelligence. Instead, the US federal government relies on a decentralized, sector-specific approach that prioritizes innovation while addressing harms through existing laws.

Key components of the US federal strategy include:

  • Executive Action: The White House issued the Executive Order on Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy AI. This order directs federal agencies to establish safety standards, protect consumer privacy, and prevent algorithmic discrimination, primarily utilizing the Defense Production Act to require safety testing data from major AI developers.
  • Voluntary Frameworks: The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) developed the AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF). While widely adopted as an industry benchmark, compliance with the NIST framework remains entirely voluntary.
  • Existing Agency Enforcement: Rather than creating a new AI regulator, the US empowers existing agencies to police AI within their specific domains. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) prosecutes deceptive AI practices and unfair competition, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulates AI in medical devices, and the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) monitors algorithmic bias in hiring.

The US State-Level Patchwork

In the absence of overarching federal legislation, individual US states have stepped in to regulate AI, resulting in a complex patchwork of local laws. State legislatures are passing both issue-specific bills and broader risk-management frameworks.

  • Comprehensive State Laws: Colorado passed the Colorado AI Act, which mirrors aspects of the EU approach by regulating "high-risk" AI systems to prevent algorithmic discrimination in consequential decisions like housing, finance, and employment.
  • Sector and Issue-Specific Laws: California, Utah, and Tennessee have enacted targeted laws. California focuses heavily on data privacy, automated decision-making tools, and transparency for generative AI training data. Tennessee passed the ELVIS Act to protect musicians' voices from unauthorized AI cloning, while Utah requires clear disclosures when consumers interact with generative AI in regulated occupations.

Key Differences in Governance and Compliance

The divergence between the US and EU approaches creates distinct operational environments for businesses developing or deploying AI technologies.

  • Uniformity vs. Fragmentation: The EU offers a single set of rules for a massive market, providing long-term legal certainty. Conversely, companies operating in the US must navigate shifting federal agency guidelines alongside dozens of varying state laws.
  • Ex-Ante vs. Ex-Post Enforcement: The EU model is largely ex-ante (preventative), requiring high-risk AI systems to prove compliance and safety before they can be deployed. The US model is predominantly ex-post (reactive), allowing rapid deployment of AI technologies and relying on regulators or courts to punish actual harms after they occur.
  • Scope of Application: The EU AI Act has extraterritorial reach, meaning US companies must comply with EU rules if their AI systems affect citizens within the European Union. US state laws generally only apply to businesses operating within or targeting residents of that specific state.
