How Thermal AI Validates Wildlife Overpass Genetics

This article explores how road ecology engineers combine thermal imaging and trajectory artificial intelligence to evaluate the success of wildlife overpasses. By tracking the exact movement patterns of elusive apex predators like grizzly bears and cougars, researchers can determine if these structures actually facilitate the cross-highway breeding necessary to restore fragmented gene pools.

Highways carve through natural habitats, creating isolated islands of wildlife. For large carnivores like grizzly bears and cougars, this fragmentation is devastating. Without the ability to safely cross highways to find new mates, isolated populations suffer from inbreeding and a lack of genetic diversity. While wildlife overpasses are built to solve this issue, engineers must prove that the animals are not just looking at the bridges, but actually using them to migrate and breed.

Traditional camera traps rely on motion sensors and infrared flashes. However, the sudden flash or the faint clicking sound of a standard camera can startle wary predators, causing them to turn back. To solve this, road ecology engineers install thermal wildlife-crossing cameras. These cameras detect heat signatures rather than visible light, allowing them to record in complete darkness, heavy snow, and dense fog without emitting a single sound or flash.

While thermal cameras capture the presence of an animal, trajectory artificial intelligence (AI) interprets their behavior. Counting the number of animals near a bridge is not enough; engineers need to know if the animal successfully crossed. Trajectory AI analyzes the thermal video feeds frame by frame, mapping the exact path, speed, and posture of a grizzly bear or cougar from the moment it approaches the overpass to the moment it exits the other side.

This AI-driven movement data reveals critical insights into predator psychology and structural success:

  • Hesitation and Rejection: If an animal approaches the overpass, stops, and turns around, the AI flags a failed crossing. This tells engineers that highway noise, light pollution, or poor bridge landscaping is deterring the animal.
  • Complete Traversals: The AI confirms when an animal walks completely across the structure, logging a successful habitat link.
  • Demographic Tracking: Advanced AI models can estimate the size and age of the thermal signatures, helping researchers identify whether solitary males, breeding females, or mothers with cubs are using the bridge.

Confirming that an animal crossed a bridge still leaves one major question: did that crossing result in genetic mixing? Engineers bridge the gap between AI technology and biology by combining thermal tracking with non-invasive genetic sampling.

Along the exact pathways identified by the trajectory AI, researchers set up hair snares—often made of barbed wire wrapped around rubbing posts or trees lured with scent. When a cougar or grizzly bear follows its tracked trajectory, it leaves behind a small tuft of hair.

Biologists collect this hair and extract the DNA. By analyzing these genetic markers, they can identify the exact individual that crossed the bridge. Over time, researchers build a genetic family tree of the predators on both sides of the highway. When the DNA of a cub born on the north side of a highway matches a male father tracked crossing from the south side via thermal AI, engineers have definitive proof of gene flow.

Through the combination of silent thermal monitoring, intelligent movement tracking, and DNA collection, road ecology engineers can accurately measure the return on investment for multi-million-dollar wildlife crossings. This data not only proves that current overpasses are saving grizzly bear and cougar populations from genetic isolation, but it also provides the exact blueprints needed to design better wildlife bridges in the future.
