Fodor's Language of Thought in Cognitive AI

Jerry Fodor’s Language of Thought Hypothesis (LOTH) revolutionized cognitive science and artificial intelligence by proposing that thinking occurs in an internal, brain-based language called "Mentalese." Introduced in his 1975 book, The Language of Thought, Fodor argued that mental representations are structured like sentences made of discrete symbols. By combining these symbols using internal grammar rules, the mind processes information mechanically, providing the theoretical foundation for classical, symbolic artificial intelligence.

At the core of Fodor's hypothesis is the Computational Theory of Mind. Fodor proposed that cognitive processes—such as believing, desiring, and deciding—are computational operations performed on mental representations. Just as a traditional computer processes data by manipulating binary code according to software algorithms, the human brain processes thoughts by manipulating internal symbols. In this view, to have a thought is to have a specific symbolic structure written in the "memory" of the mind.

For these internal symbols to function like a language, Fodor argued they must possess two critical properties: syntax and semantics. Syntax refers to the physical structure or shape of the symbols and the grammatical rules that govern how they can be arranged. Semantics refers to the actual meaning of the symbols—how they point to objects, properties, or events in the real world.

In cognitive AI, this distinction is vital because machines cannot inherently "understand" meaning; they can only respond to physical or mathematical structures. Fodor solved this by stating that the mind's syntax parallels its semantics. Because the physical rules of the symbols match the logical rules of their meanings, a system can manipulate symbols based purely on their syntax (shape and rule) and still arrive at a meaningful, truth-preserving conclusion. This is the exact mechanism behind automated reasoning and logic programming in AI.

Fodor claimed that this internal symbolic code is the only way to explain three unique features of intelligence:

  • Compositionality: Complex thoughts are built from simpler, reusable atomic concepts. For example, the mental symbol for "brown" and the symbol for "dog" combine to form the thought "brown dog."
  • Productivity: By using a finite set of symbols and grammatical rules, an intelligent system can generate an infinite number of novel thoughts.
  • Systematicity: The ability to think one thought is intrinsically tied to the ability to think logically related thoughts. If an AI system has the symbolic capacity to represent "John loves Mary," it automatically has the structural capacity to represent "Mary loves John" simply by rearranging the symbols.

In the realm of artificial intelligence, Fodor’s hypothesis became the philosophical backbone of Symbolic AI, often referred to as GOFAI (Good Old-Fashioned Artificial Intelligence). Under this paradigm, AI researchers built systems like expert systems, knowledge graphs, and logic solvers. These programs operate on explicit symbols (words, numbers, logic gates) and use "if-then" rules to simulate human decision-making.

Through the Language of Thought, Fodor demonstrated how abstract concepts like beliefs and logic could be physically realized in a mechanical system. By treating the mind as a symbol-manipulating engine, his hypothesis bridged the gap between human psychology and computer science, establishing a blueprint for how machines can be programmed to reason.
