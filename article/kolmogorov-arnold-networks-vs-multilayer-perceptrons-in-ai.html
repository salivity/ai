Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks vs. Multilayer Perceptrons in AI

This article explores the fundamental differences between Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) and traditional Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) in artificial intelligence. It examines their distinct mathematical foundations, structural architectures, and how they process data. Furthermore, the text highlights the practical implications of these differences, focusing on parameter efficiency, model interpretability, and computational performance.

Mathematical Foundations

The primary difference between MLPs and KANs lies in the mathematical theorems that inspire them.

Traditional MLPs are based on the Universal Approximation Theorem. This theorem states that a neural network with a single hidden layer containing a finite number of neurons can approximate any continuous function, provided the activation function is non-linear.

KANs are based on the Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Theorem. This theorem states that any multivariate continuous function can be represented as a superposition of continuous functions of a single variable and addition. Instead of learning linear combinations of fixed non-linearities, KANs learn the non-linear functions themselves.

Architectural Differences: Nodes vs. Edges

The structural layout of nodes (neurons) and edges (connections) is inverted between the two architectures.

In a traditional MLP:

  • Edges hold learnable weights (linear parameters).
  • Nodes apply fixed non-linear activation functions (such as ReLU, Sigmoid, or GELU) to the summed inputs.

In a Kolmogorov-Arnold Network:

  • Edges contain learnable activation functions. These functions are typically parameterized as B-splines, which are piecewise polynomial functions that can adapt their shape during training.
  • Nodes simply sum the incoming signals without applying any non-linear activation or learnable weights.

Parameter Efficiency and Scaling

Because KANs learn the activation functions directly on the edges, they can capture complex data patterns using significantly fewer parameters than MLPs. An MLP often requires thousands of wide and deep layers to approximate a function that a KAN can represent with just a few nodes.

In tasks involving mathematical laws, physics simulations, and symbolic regression, KANs achieve higher accuracy with a fraction of the network size required by MLPs. This makes KANs highly parameter-efficient for specific scientific domains.

Interpretability

MLPs are widely considered "black boxes." Because knowledge is distributed across millions of linear weights and fixed activations, it is extremely difficult for humans to understand the exact mathematical formula an MLP has learned.

KANs offer much higher interpretability. Since the network is generally smaller and uses spline-based activation functions on the edges, researchers can visualize the learned functions directly. After training, a KAN can often be pruned and translated into a human-readable symbolic mathematical formula, making it easier to see exactly how the AI arrived at its conclusion.

Training and Computational Trade-offs

While KANs excel in parameter efficiency and interpretability, MLPs currently hold an advantage in raw training speed and hardware optimization.

MLPs rely heavily on simple matrix multiplications. Modern hardware, particularly GPUs and TPUs, is specifically engineered to execute matrix multiplications at massive scale and high speed.

KANs must evaluate and update B-spline functions on every edge during training. This process is computationally heavier per parameter than the simple weight multiplication in MLPs. Consequently, a KAN may take longer to train than an MLP of comparable parameter size, though ongoing research is focused on optimizing KAN operations for modern hardware.

Continual Learning and Catastrophic Forgetting

MLPs suffer from "catastrophic forgetting." When an MLP is trained on a new task, the global updates to its weights often overwrite the data it learned from previous tasks.

KANs handle continual learning more effectively due to the local nature of splines. Because a B-spline only affects a specific local region of the input space, a KAN can learn new data patterns by adjusting splines in one region without disrupting the already-learned functions in other regions.
